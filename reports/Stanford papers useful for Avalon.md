# Stanford speeds up the agent, not the Avalon kernels

Of the roughly 75 Stanford papers from Nov. 2022 - Nov. 2026 covered in the notes, **none brings a decode speedup ready to port** to our configuration (Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, ~125B hybrid GDN + attention MoE, MTP head, experts offloaded to RAM, a single RX 7900 XT). What they bring instead is solid evidence for three levers we already control and that weigh more than a kernel: **how many thinking tokens the agent spends** (s1, Large Language Monkeys, ALP), **how much prefill we redo on every turn** (RadixAttention from SGLang, Lost in the Middle, ACE) and **how we measure** (Terminal-Bench, HELM, Intelligence per Watt). Concretely, opencode now runs with default effort `xhigh`, and the Stanford literature supports a `low`/`medium` default with escalation only on failure, plus an s1-style cap on thinking tokens. At roughly 95 t/s on prose, every 1,000 thinking tokens avoided means about 10.5 s saved per turn, with no line of code in the engine. On the server side, the valuable idea is a cache with **several prompt states** (with snapshots of the GDN recurrent state at message boundaries), which confirms and extends recommendations #24891/#30745/#28092 from the upstream report. The only new and plausible engine idea is **CATS** (activation sparsity on the experts computed on the CPU), with an estimated gain of 20-30% on CPU expert time, but unverified on MoE. The Stanford kernels (FlashAttention, ThunderKittens, HipKittens) are either already present in llama.cpp or tied to NVIDIA or CDNA. All numbers below are the authors', obtained on other hardware: the model is missing from the disk and `avalon.service` is stopped, so nothing has been measured on our machine yet.

## Reading criteria: 20 GB VRAM, a single slot, expensive prefill

The research notes covered four areas: speculative decoding and serving, attention kernels and architectures, quantization/MoE/offload, plus agents and NLP. Each paper below has a role marker. **Lead** means the first or last author is at Stanford or that the paper comes from a Stanford lab (Hazy Research / Ré, Scaling Intelligence / Mirhoseini, CRFM / Liang, Stanford NLP, the Zou group). **Co** means Stanford appears only among the co-authors. Where the affiliation was inferred from lab membership and not read on the page, I mark it explicitly as "inferred affiliation".

The applicability verdict starts from four facts about the configuration. First: decode is limited by expert reads from RAM, not by GPU compute. Second: there is a single slot and a single user, so everything that gains from large batches (FlexGen, Hydragen, Tokasaurus, Weaver) loses almost all its advantage. Third: the GDN layers have recurrent state, which cannot be truncated to an arbitrary prefix, so any prefix-reuse idea needs state snapshots. Fourth: opencode sends prompts of 30-130k tokens, so prefill and TTFT dominate turn latency. Besides these, a few things were already tested on this machine without gain: HIP graphs, the unroll flag, the COMPUTE power profile, draft p-min, checkpoint tuning, Vulkan and KV turbo4/TurboQuant. I do not recommend them again without a new reason ([upstream report](file:///home/dan/reports/Avalon%20upgrades%20from%20upstream%20projects.md)).

Papers led by other institutions but mentioned in the notes are listed here only for traceability and belong to the next reports in the series. This is the case for QuIP#/QTIP (Cornell), GEPA (Berkeley, with Stanford co-authors), Gated DeltaNet and flash-linear-attention (MIT/NVIDIA), FlashAttention-3 (Princeton/Together/Colfax/Meta/NVIDIA), TEAL (MIT/Together/Princeton), as well as the expert caching and prefetching literature (Yandex, Edinburgh, UW, Berkeley).

## Serving: the prefix cache matters more than throughput

Stanford co-founded almost all of the modern serving literature, but almost all of it targets throughput across many users. For a single slot, what transfers is the idea, not the code.

| Paper | Stanford role | What it does | Verdict for Avalon |
|---|---|---|---|
| SGLang / RadixAttention ([arXiv 2312.07104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104)) | Lead (Ying Sheng, last author; Kozyrakis, Barrett co-authors) | Radix tree of KV prefixes with LRU eviction; up to 6.4x throughput | **High as an idea**: several cached prompt states, with GDN snapshots |
| FlexGen ([arXiv 2303.06865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06865)) | Lead (Sheng, first author; Liang, Ré) | Linear program for placing tensors on GPU/CPU/disk; OPT-175B at 1 tok/s with effective batch 144 | Small: only the cost-model idea for placement; at batch 1 the gain is ~0 |
| H2O ([arXiv 2306.14048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14048)) | Co (lead UT Austin) | KV eviction: ~20% "heavy-hitter" tokens + the recent ones | **No**: the hybrid's KV is already small, and eviction loses exactly the code tokens |
| Hydragen ([arXiv 2402.05099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05099)) | Lead | Separate attention on shared prefix / suffixes; up to 32x throughput | No: needs many sequences with a shared prefix |
| Tokasaurus ([code](https://github.com/ScalingIntelligence/tokasaurus)) | Lead | Throughput engine, over 3x versus vLLM/SGLang | No |
| Cartridges ([arXiv 2506.06266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06266)) | Lead | KV trained offline per corpus; 38.6x less memory | No: needs backprop through the 125B model |
| Auditing Prompt Caching in Language Model APIs ([arXiv 2502.07776](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07776)) | Lead | The hit/miss timing difference is a side channel | No gain; partition the cache if more users appear |
| Minions ([arXiv 2502.15964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15964)) | Lead (Hazy) | The local model reads the long context, the large one plans | An agent workflow option, not a speed one |
| Archon ([arXiv 2409.15254](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15254)) | Lead | Search over ensembles of inference techniques | No: multiplies LLM calls |

**RadixAttention is the only paper in the group that changes something concrete.** Opencode alternates the main agent, sub-agents and summary or compaction requests, each with a different prefix. With a single cached state, every switch forces a long re-prefill. A small LRU store with several states in RAM, or on the D drive for older states, would turn switches into prefix hits ([arXiv 2312.07104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104)). On a hybrid model, the mandatory adaptation is that states are saved at chosen boundaries (end of the system prompt, end of each user message), not per token, because the GDN state cannot be truncated. That is exactly what #30745 (the last-user-end checkpoint replaces earlier checkpoints, reuse from 91% to 94%) and #28092 (persistent, hybrid-aware prompt cache on disk, which removes a ~70 s cold prefill at 131k) already do. Both appear in the [upstream report](file:///home/dan/reports/Avalon%20upgrades%20from%20upstream%20projects.md). The Stanford paper **supports that recommendation and extends it** from "one correct checkpoint" to "several entries, with LRU eviction". The difference matters for opencode sub-agents, which otherwise evict each other. One local detail partly shifts the priority: opencode's `small_model` was moved to `opencode-go/glm-5.3-flash` (cloud), so title requests no longer hit Avalon, and #30188 becomes less urgent ([Avalon memory](file:///home/dan/.claude/projects/-home-dan/memory/project_avalon.md)).

FlexGen deserves a mention for its method, not its planner. The idea of solving placement with a linear program over measured bandwidths, instead of tuning it by hand with `-ot` regexes, overlaps with the hosted-MoE limit computed from the link rate, already present in the new commits in Avalon main. The remaining gain is probably small.

## Speculative decoding: two portable ideas, both with modest gain

Stanford has two speculative decoding papers of its own in the window, plus a megakernel experiment. **None beats the MTP + ngram-simple configuration on a single GPU**, which already gives 168-181 t/s on code edits.

| Paper | Stanford role | Essence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staged Speculative Decoding ([arXiv 2308.04623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04623)) | Lead (Spector, Ré) | Draft tree + a second speculative level for the drafter; 3.16x lower latency on GPT-2-L | Partial: a small tree, top-2 on the first 1-2 positions; ~5-15% estimated |
| Speculative Speculative Decoding / Saguaro ([arXiv 2603.03251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03251), [code](https://github.com/tanishqkumar/ssd)) | Lead (first author Tanishq Kumar, Stanford; with Dao, May) | Precomputes the next draft for likely verification outcomes; ~30% over the best SD | The "SSD-lite" variant with the ngram drafter on the CPU; 0-10% estimated |
| Look Ma, No Bubbles! (Llama-1B megakernel) ([blog](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles)) | Lead (Hazy) | The whole forward pass in one persistent kernel; 78% of H100 bandwidth | Not as an implementation; the lesson about "bubbles" gives no new reason for HIP graphs |
| Next-Concept Modeling ([arXiv 2610.14162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14162)) | Lead | Coarse-to-fine parallel generation | No: needs a specially trained model |
| SPRINT ([arXiv 2506.05745](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05745)) | Lead | Reasoning with parallel branches | No: fine-tuning + batch > 1 |

SSD has a setup that does not match ours: the drafter runs on **a separate, dedicated H100**, and the authors note that the fallback on cache miss returns random tokens and that an n-gram speculator would do better ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.03251)). That very remark makes our variant portable. While the GPU verifies, free CPU threads can prepare ngram drafts for the most likely bonus tokens and for the "accept all" and "accept n-1" outcomes. The limit is hard: the gain cannot exceed the host time between GPU steps, and our CPU is already busy with the missed experts. That is why the gap between steps must first be measured with a profiler.

The Staged SD tree has a specific cost on our architecture. Each branch activates different experts (so more reads from RAM), and the GDN state must be forked per branch. Upstream has a tree speculation variant in progress (#30931) and suffix decoding (#26283), listed as "to watch" in the [upstream report](file:///home/dan/reports/Avalon%20upgrades%20from%20upstream%20projects.md). The recommendation stays the same: we follow those PRs, we do not write our own variant.

About the megakernel: the post shows that, at batch 1, kernel launch overhead can dominate ([blog](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles)). One of the notes proposes a "HIP graphs audit" on this basis. HIP graphs were already tested here without gain, though, and Avalon runs the hosted MoE inside the HIP graph. **I do not recommend repeating the test.** The only valid reason would be a profile showing that the gaps between kernels dominate the 17-32-token verification steps. That measurement is part of the SSD-lite profiling anyway.

Stanford has no paper on lookahead/Jacobi decoding, Medusa, EAGLE or SuffixDecoding. Those belong to LMSYS/UCSD, Princeton/UIUC/CMU, PKU/Waterloo and Snowflake/CMU, so to the next reports.

## Test-time compute: the default `xhigh` effort contradicts the evidence

This is the group with the best gain/effort ratio for us, because the intervention happens in the agent configuration and the server, not in kernels.

| Paper | Stanford role | Key result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| s1: Simple test-time scaling ([arXiv 2501.19393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393)) | Lead (Liang, Hashimoto, Candès; with UW/AI2) | Budget forcing: AIME24 rises from 50% to 57%; s1-32B beats o1-preview by up to 27% | **Directly applicable**: thinking token cap with forced `</think>` |
| Large Language Monkeys ([arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)) | Lead (Ré, Mirhoseini) | SWE-bench Lite: 15.9% at 1 sample, 56% at 250; without a verifier, the gain plateaus | The principle: test-verified retries > a single long attempt |
| CodeMonkeys ([arXiv 2501.14723](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14723)) | Lead | 57.4% on SWE-bench Verified; code context shared across samples | Supports a strong prefix cache, for cheap retries |
| Just Enough Thinking / ALP ([arXiv 2506.05256](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05256v1)) | Lead (affiliation from snippet) | Length penalty inversely proportional to the solve rate | The inference analog: short thinking on easy turns |
| How Do Large Language Monkeys Get Their Power (Laws)? ([arXiv 2502.17578](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17578)) | Lead | Theory of coverage power laws | No practical implications |
| Weaver ([arXiv 2506.18203](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18203)) | Lead | Combined weak verifiers | No: many samples |
| Think, Prune, Train, Improve ([arXiv 2504.18116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18116)) | Lead | Training method | No |
| Quiet-STaR ([arXiv 2403.09629](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09629)) | Lead | Learned token-level rationales | No (training) |
| Cognitive Behaviors that Enable Self-Improving Reasoners ([arXiv 2503.01307](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01307)) | Lead | Qwen already has verification and backtracking | Cheap prompt lever: "check the result of the last tool" |

The practical conclusion comes from the current configuration, not only from theory. Opencode runs with default effort `xhigh` ([Avalon memory](file:///home/dan/.claude/projects/-home-dan/memory/project_avalon.md)), and most turns of a coding agent are of the "read the result, emit the next call" kind. ALP shows that short thinking on easy prompts loses little, and s1 shows that accuracy rises monotonically with the budget but saturates ([arXiv 2501.19393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393), [arXiv 2506.05256](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05256v1)). The resulting policy: `low` by default on routine turns, `medium`/`xhigh` only for planning, debugging hard failures and designing the final patch, with automatic escalation on a tool error, failed tests or a repeated identical action. This is an extrapolation: **no Stanford paper evaluated effort levels on agentic coding turns**, so the policy must be validated with the harness from the measurement section.

Budget forcing has two sides, and for us only the cap matters. Extending with "Wait" adds latency and is worth trying at most on hard problems, in a single step. The cap is simple to implement: at N thinking tokens, the server injects `</think>` and continues in answer mode. Upstream has `--reasoning-budget` (classically only 0 or unlimited), a soft wrap-up (#27578) and, in Strata, `reasoning_close_retry` and thinking skip for short answers (the median drops from 0.72 to 0.50 s), all listed in the [upstream report](file:///home/dan/reports/Avalon%20upgrades%20from%20upstream%20projects.md). s1 **changes the priority** of those items: from "to watch", the per-effort-level cap becomes an immediate action, because it is the paper that shows a hard cap is principled, not just a hack. Monkeys and CodeMonkeys move the rest of the compute budget from thinking into retries: when there is a test command, 2-3 sequential low-effort attempts beat a single `xhigh` attempt, and the prefix cache makes each retry cost only decode ([arXiv 2407.21787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)).

The mechanism that links these papers to our speed: llama.cpp reuses the KV only up to the first changed token. A byte-stable system prompt, with the tool schemas and an optional project playbook at the top, with no timestamps or variable state in the prefix, and with new turns appended at the tail, keeps the cache warm. Lost in the Middle adds something unrelated to the cache: models use information at the start and end of the context best, so a short "current task / next step" block in the last user message helps quality without breaking the cache ([arXiv 2307.03172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). There is no Stanford paper yet that measures this effect on 2025-2026 reasoning models at 128k+, so its size is uncertain. On the cache side, the recommendation matches `env_to_user` from Strata, already listed upstream.

ACE is the paper that should change compaction. Repeatedly rewriting the whole history into a single summary erodes details ("context collapse" and "brevity bias" in the paper's terms). An itemized summary (files touched, decisions, failed tests, TODOs, exact error strings), updated by deltas and triggered rarely, at fixed points, keeps more and invalidates the cache less often ([arXiv 2510.04618](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04618v1)). Dynamic Cheatsheet completes the idea with a project-level "lessons learned" file, loaded in the stable prefix, which grows through clean deltas between sessions ([arXiv 2504.07952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07952v1)). No Stanford paper explicitly deals with compaction for coding agents, so the transfer is an extrapolation.

DSPy Assertions offers a simple model for malformed tool calls: retry with the error attached. It is the same family as the tolerant XML tool-call recovery in Strata (98.3% versus 92.9% turns read correctly), already recommended in the upstream report. The Stanford paper strengthens the recommendation without adding new code. Automatic prompt optimization (MIPROv2 from DSPy) is justified only on sub-prompts with a metric, first of all the compaction prompt, with Avalon as the reflection LM, run offline, at night. GEPA, the more sample-efficient variant, is led by Berkeley and will be covered there. The fine-tuning flow for helpers (Alpaca, then KTO on automatic success/failure labels from tools, with ReFT as a lighter alternative to LoRA) makes sense only if the local 2B/4B helpers come back into use. Since `small_model` is now a cloud model, I put it at low priority.

The kernel note concluded that the biggest opportunity on RDNA3 would be a chunked GDN kernel on WMMA, based on a single report from gfx1151 where the HIP GDN ran at CPU speed ([issue #20354, mirror](https://2.zoppoz.workers.dev/https/github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/20354)). The upstream report shows, however, that **Avalon already has a chunked GDN prefill on WMMA for RDNA3 and fused qwen4exp kernels** ([upstream report](file:///home/dan/reports/Avalon%20upgrades%20from%20upstream%20projects.md)). The recommendation becomes a measurement: what fraction of the 131k prefill the GDN and full-attention layers take versus the experts. Only if one of them dominates is kernel work worth it.

## Quantization, MoE and sparsity: CATS is the only new engine idea

| Paper | Stanford role | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| CATS ([arXiv 2404.08763](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08763v4)) | Lead (Mirhoseini) | **The best engine idea**: threshold on \|SiLU(gate)\| and up/down computed only for the active neurons on the CPU experts; 20-30% estimated on CPU expert time |
| Deja Vu ([arXiv 2310.17157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17157)) | Co (lead Rice/CMU) | Superseded by CATS, which needs no trained predictors |
| Scaling Laws for Precision ([arXiv 2411.04330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330)) | Co (lead Harvard; Ré, Spector, Muennighoff, inferred affiliation) | Guide: models trained on many tokens are more fragile under PTQ, so more bits for attention/router/shared experts |
| CALDERA ([arXiv 2405.18886](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18886v2)), LPLR ([arXiv 2310.11028](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11028)) | Lead (Pilanci) | Competitive only below ~2.5 bpw; new tensor type + E8 kernels; low priority |
| MegaBlocks ([arXiv 2211.15841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15841)) | Lead (Zaharia) | Training-oriented; `mul_mat_id` already covers batch 1; ~0 |
| SambaNova SN40L / CoE ([arXiv 2405.07518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07518)) | Co (Olukotun, industry) | Conceptually validates tiered expert memory; no portable algorithm |
| FlexGen 4-bit ([arXiv 2303.06865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06865)) | Lead | Weaker than IQ3_KS/q4_0; no gain |

CATS reports 50% activation sparsity in the MLP with negligible loss on Mistral-7B and Llama-2-7B and a ~15% gain in generation latency on GPU, with a dedicated kernel ([arXiv 2404.08763](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08763v4)). For us, the idea applies where it hurts: the missed experts, computed on the 5700G CPU. Gate is computed first, then the threshold is applied, and up and down are computed only for the neurons that passed the threshold. For up, rows can be skipped directly. For down, a re-pack at load time in n_ff-major order is needed, so that whole quantization blocks can be skipped. The notes' estimate, ~1/3 fewer bytes and FLOPs per CPU expert, so 20-30% of CPU expert time, is an inference. The main risk is that fine-grained MoE experts (small n_ff) may be less sparse than dense 7B MLPs, **something no Stanford paper tested**. The first step must therefore be a cheap offline measurement of the |SiLU(gate)| distribution on the Qwen experts, and only then the 1-2 week AVX2 kernel.

Scaling Laws for Precision brings a free diagnostic. 89 GiB for ~125B parameters means ~6.1 bpw on average, well above the nominal ~3.25 bpw of IQ3_KS. So either the parameter count is higher, or a large share of the tensors is already at high precision. A `gguf-dump` of the tensor type distribution settles the question and shows whether the bit allocation follows the paper's guide (high precision on attention, router and shared experts, low precision on cold routed experts). The calculation is arithmetic on the figures from the brief, not a sourced fact. Requantization makes sense only if a clear deviation shows up, and the GGUF files belong to the Avalon maintainer.

**The literature on expert offloading, caching, prefetching and router prediction has no Stanford-led papers.** The papers relevant to Avalon (Eliseev & Mazur, MoE-Infinity, Fiddler, MoE-Lightning, fMoE, SSD-LLaMA, REAM) belong to the next reports. So does QuIP#/QTIP from Cornell, whose line shows up, according to the notes and unverified, in the IQ*_KT types in ik_llama.

All actions in the next section that affect quality (lower effort, thinking cap, new compaction, CATS) need a comparable baseline. Terminal-Bench 2.x has 89 hand-verified tasks, and frontier agents solve under 65% ([arXiv 2601.11868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11868v1)). A fixed subset, run with opencode on Avalon at each effort level, gives the success rate, time per task, tokens and number of compactions. HELM gives the framework: a change that raises accuracy but doubles turn time can be a net loss in interactive use. I have not checked whether Harbor has an opencode adapter. It supports custom agents, but the integration effort is unknown. Intelligence per Watt evaluated neither RDNA3 GPUs nor a 100B+ MoE with experts on the CPU, and the authors argue that local hardware favors small dense models over sparse MoEs ([Hazy retrospective](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2026-05-15-minions-to-openjarvis-retrospective)). That is exactly the opposite of our configuration, so their conclusions do not transfer directly, even if the harness can be reused.

## Conclusion

The Stanford papers move the question "how do we make Avalon faster" from tokens per second to seconds per agent turn. The labs that invented FlashAttention, RadixAttention and FlexGen produced nothing that beats MTP + ngram on a consumer GPU with experts in RAM. Their papers on test-time compute and context show, however, that a coding agent can lose more time thinking needlessly and redoing prefill than decoding. That is why the first four actions do not touch kernels and can be done in a few days after the model is restored. The only truly new engine bet, CATS on the CPU experts, must be treated as an experiment: a one-afternoon sparsity measurement decides whether the two weeks are worth it.

The second gain of this report is a map of the gaps. Stanford has no papers on expert offload, router prediction, KV quantization, draft-free decoding or kernels for the gated delta rule. That is exactly where most of our decode time goes. The next reports (MIT for Gated DeltaNet, TEAL and lattice-type quantization, Berkeley for GEPA and KVQuant, then the European and Asian groups for expert caching) will probably have more to say about the engine than Stanford did.
