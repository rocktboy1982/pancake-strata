# Disaggregation wins clusters, not a single PC

The LLM runtime architectures of 2026 come down to one idea applied at four scales: **separate the things that get in each other's way**. Prefill (compute-bound) is separated from decode (bandwidth-bound), with the KV cache shipped between them over NIXL or the Mooncake Transfer Engine. The control plane (scheduler, KV manager, request lifecycle) is separated from the execution plane (kernels, forward pass). The service layer (instance pools, global KV index) is separated from the engine specific to each accelerator. And the policy for "how long the model should think" is separated from decoding itself. The AI-written overview you pasted is **mostly right on names and less right on details**: llm-d 1.0, TokenSpeed, xLLM with Mooncake, and Exo (arXiv:2611.00463) all exist. But the real xLLM repository is `jd-opensource/xllm`, not "xLLM-AI". "Zero runtime errors" for TokenSpeed appears in no primary source. Exo is a runtime for self-improving *agents*, not an inference engine, and "up to 53%" is the top of only one of four reported ranges (12-82% overall). "Runtime intelligence" is not an established term. For pancake-strata (one user, one RX 7900 XT, ~126 tok/s exact), physical P/D disaggregation, chunked-prefill interleaving and reasoning-aware schedulers bring nothing, because there are no concurrent requests to arbitrate between. What transfers is the *inside-one-token* version of the same ideas. Concretely: overlapping CPU experts (~21% of the window) with GPU work through Expert Deferral, folding MTP drafting (~9%) into the captured graph, a cache policy aware of turn boundaries, and a thinking budget. The engine already has most of the foundations.

## Four families, one separation principle

### P/D disaggregation and KV streaming solve interference between requests

The motivation is physical. Prefill processes all prompt tokens in parallel and is compute-bound. Decode produces one token per step and rereads the weights plus the whole KV cache, so it is memory-bound. When both phases share one GPU, a long prefill blocks other requests' decode steps. The Hao AI Lab retrospective shows that **even with chunked prefill, a single large prefill can inflate TPOT by 2-30x under bursty load**, and colocation forces the allocator to provision "for the worst case of both metrics simultaneously" ([Hao AI Lab](https://haoailab.com/blogs/distserve-retro)). The founding papers date from 2023-2024: DistServe (OSDI'24) reports up to 7.4x more requests within SLO ([arXiv 2401.09670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670)), Microsoft's Splitwise 1.4x throughput at 20% lower cost ([arXiv 2311.18677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18677)), and Mooncake (Moonshot AI, FAST 2025 Best Paper) 75% more requests on real Kimi traffic ([arXiv 2407.00079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079)).

What changed in 2026 is **the maturing of the KV-transfer substrate**. NVIDIA Dynamo moves KV directly from prefill VRAM to decode VRAM over NIXL (NVLink or InfiniBand/UCX), without blocking. The SGLang backend lets decode start during the transfer, while vLLM and TRT-LLM run prefill synchronously ([Dynamo design doc](https://docs.nvidia.com/dynamo/v1.2.0/design-docs/disaggregated-serving.md)). The Mooncake Transfer Engine covers TCP, RDMA, EFA, NVMe-oF, NVLink, HIP, CXL and Ascend, and **reaches 87 GB/s on 4x200 Gbps RoCE and 190 GB/s on 8x400 Gbps**. It has become a shared component used by vLLM, SGLang, Dynamo, TensorRT-LLM, LMCache and xLLM ([Mooncake](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/Mooncake)). In vLLM, the connectors (NixlConnector, MooncakeConnector, MoRIIOConnector on ROCm only, LMCache, OffloadingConnector) are configured via `--kv-transfer-config`, and workers load and store KV layer by layer so the transfer overlaps attention ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/features/disagg_prefill/)).

On the layer above, **llm-d 1.0 (20 October 2026)** is a Kubernetes-native control plane orchestrating vLLM, SGLang, AMD ATOM and TorchTPU. It is not an engine. The release brings a "DisaggregatedSet" that manages prefill and decode replicas as one unit with lockstep upgrades, coordination consolidated into llm-d-router, session-sticky routing, and KV transport over NIXL, MoRI-IO or TPU Sync ([llm-d v1.0](https://llm-d.ai/blog/llm-d-v1.0-state-of-the-art-inference-ready-for-day-2)). The detail that matters most: the vLLM docs say explicitly that **disaggregated prefill "DOES NOT improve throughput"**. The real gain is separate control of TTFT and ITL ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/features/disagg_prefill/)). Big figures like Dynamo's 30x for DeepSeek-R1 on GB200 NVL72 ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvidia-dynamo-a-low-latency-distributed-inference-framework-for-scaling-reasoning-ai-models)) are regime-dependent peaks: a later NVIDIA blog projects ~6x in the medium-latency regime ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-nvidia-gb200-nvl72-and-nvidia-dynamo-boost-inference-performance-for-moe-models/)). They bundle P/D with wide expert parallelism and new hardware. The llm-d partner gains are not broken out per technique either, and many come from prefix-aware routing, not P/D. Google Vertex, for example, raised its prefix hit rate from 35% to 70% ([llm-d v1.0](https://llm-d.ai/blog/llm-d-v1.0-state-of-the-art-inference-ready-for-day-2)).

### Control/execution separation became standard, with three tiers

All the major engines converged on the same pattern. A CPU scheduler prepares step N+1 while the GPU runs step N, and tokenization and detokenization leave the hot loop. The idea comes from NanoFlow, which measured CPU work at over 10% of inference time when the GPU is well utilized ([arXiv 2408.12757](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.12757v1)). SGLang v0.4's "zero-overhead" scheduler runs one batch ahead and gives **~1.1x from overlap alone** ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2024-12-04-sglang-v0-4)). TRT-LLM has an overlap scheduler on by default, at the cost of one extra decode step ([TRT-LLM](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/1.2.0/torch/features/overlap_scheduler.html)). vLLM Model Runner V2 (March 2026) builds inputs directly on the GPU with Triton and reports **+56% on Qwen3-0.6B, but only −6.3% TPOT on GLM-4.7 with MTP**. The gain shrinks as model size grows ([vLLM MRV2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)).

TokenSpeed (LightSeek Foundation, MIT, development started March 2026) takes the separation one step further. The control plane is written in C++ as a **finite-state machine (FSM) "that works with the type system"**: request lifecycle, KV ownership and overlap timing are explicit transitions, with safe KV reuse enforced "at compile time rather than at runtime". The execution plane stays in Python for development speed ([LightSeek blog](https://lightseek.org/blog/lightseek-tokenspeed.html); [GitHub](https://github.com/lightseekorg/tokenspeed)). In front sit Rust gateways (SGLang Model Gateway, Dynamo) speaking msgpack, with no Python on the request path. The full architecture thus has three tiers: Rust gateway, control plane, execution plane ([SGLang Model Gateway](https://docs.sglang.io/advanced_features/sgl_model_gateway.html)). TokenSpeed's benchmarks are its own, on a single model (Kimi K2.5 on B200) and against a single baseline (TRT-LLM): **~9% lower latency at batch 1 and ~11% higher throughput** near 100 TPS/user. In May 2026 the release was still a "performance preview" ([LightSeek blog](https://lightseek.org/blog/lightseek-tokenspeed.html)).

### Service-engine decoupling is the answer to heterogeneous accelerators

xLLM (JD.com, Apache-2.0) is the cleanest example. xLLM-Service owns three elastic pools (Prefill, Decode, Encode), dynamic PD and EPD disaggregation, online/offline co-location, and fault-tolerant global KV management. xLLM-Engine handles execution: a three-level pipeline, Adaptive Graph Mode on top of ACLGraph on Ascend with parameterized shapes, and xTensor, a "logically contiguous, physically discrete" KV manager in the style of vAttention ([arXiv 2510.14686](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14686v1)). The current README lists **Ascend, Cambricon MLU, Moore Threads MUSA, Hygon DCU, MetaX and Iluvatar**, and the project was donated to the OpenAtom Foundation in mid-2026 ([GitHub jd-opensource/xllm](https://github.com/jd-opensource/xllm)). The hierarchical HBM-DRAM-SSD KV takes its ideas from Mooncake Store and uses the Mooncake Transfer Engine ([arXiv 2510.14686](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14686v1)). The benchmarks are the authors' own and **measured only on Ascend 910B/910C**: up to 2.2x over vLLM-Ascend and 1.7x over MindIE, against baseline versions from mid-2025 ([arXiv 2510.14686](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14686v1)).

The rest of the ecosystem follows two models. The first is a common core with out-of-tree plugins: vLLM's `Platform` interface via `vllm.platform_plugins`, used by vllm-ascend, vllm-kunlun, IBM Spyre and TPU ([vLLM Hardware Plugin](https://vllm.ai/blog/2025-05-12-hardware-plugin)). The second is a portable kernel library: llama.cpp/ggml with ~17 backends, including CANN, MUSA, HIP and Vulkan ([llama.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp)), or MLC-LLM compiled through TVM. What matters for your machine is that vLLM officially lists gfx1100 (RX 7900) on ROCm 6.3+, with `rocm100` wheels ([vLLM install](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/getting_started/installation/gpu.html)). xLLM, MindIE and vllm-ascend have no AMD/HIP backend.

### "Runtime intelligence" actually means controlled inference-time compute

The term has no established technical source. The literature talks about test-time compute and reasoning-aware serving ([arXiv 2512.02008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02008)). The real mechanisms fall into three groups. The first is certainty-based early exit: Dynasor-CoT probes the answer every 32-128 tokens and stops when consecutive probes agree, with **up to 29% fewer tokens and no retraining** ([Hao AI Lab](https://haoailab.com/blogs/dynasor-cot/)). Certaindex/Dynasor reports up to 50% compute saved ([arXiv 2412.20993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20993)). The second is a hard thinking budget enforced from the logits: `thinking_token_budget` with `reasoning_end_str` in vLLM ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/reasoning_outputs.html)) and `--reasoning-budget` in llama.cpp, implemented as a state machine ([reasoning-budget.h](https://huggingface.co/datasets/echodict/llama.cpp/blob/main/common/reasoning-budget.h)). The third is step-level speculation: SpecReason, 1.4-3.0x ([arXiv 2504.07891](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07891)). On top of these comes KV compression for long traces (R-KV, ThinKV). There is a caveat here: aggressive eviction can *lengthen* the reasoning trace and cancel the savings ([arXiv 2512.12008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12008)). Qwen3 tolerates truncated thinking through "Thinking Mode Fusion", so a budget is safer on the Qwen family than on models without this property ([Qwen3 Technical Report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.09388)).

Exo sits one level higher. The paper arXiv:2611.00463 ("Exo: An Architecture for Self-Improving Systems at Runtime", Krentsel, Ratnasamy, Stoica, Casado, Goyal, 30 Oct 2026) describes an agent harness that edits its own policies during execution. A "minimal protected mechanism layer" preserves execution history and governs policy activation ([arXiv:2611.00463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00463)). The savings come from fewer or cheaper model calls, not from faster decoding. It is unrelated to exo-explore/exo, the distributed engine for Mac clusters.

## Published gains assume concurrency and an RDMA network

Nearly every big number in this area is "up to", self-reported and obtained on clusters. P/D disaggregation wins when there are many users whose decodes must be protected from other users' prefills, long prompts, and interconnects of tens of GB/s. Practitioner guides (unofficial heuristics) say to avoid it on a single GPU, where "there is nowhere to send the KV cache", below ~4 GPUs, and for prompts under ~512 tokens ([BentoML](https://origin.bentoml.com/llm/inference-optimization/prefill-decode-disaggregation); [dev.to](https://dev.to/ji_ai/prefilldecode-disaggregation-stop-serving-llms-on-one-gpu-461h)). The local measurement is harsher than the heuristic: **Strata's split serving (prefill on one card, decode on another, session handed over via RAM) lost on all 6 workloads on 2x RTX A6000**. It was 1.16x slower at 8K, 1.48x at 32K, and 1.04-1.30x on chats (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/docs/SPLIT_SERVE.md`).

Control/execution overlap follows the same rule. Its gain is proportional to the share of host overhead: 1.56x on a 0.6B model, single digits on large models ([vLLM MRV2](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-03-24-mrv2)). TRT-LLM's version costs one extra decode step, which conflicts with speculative verification, where the next window depends on acceptance ([TRT-LLM](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/1.2.0/torch/features/overlap_scheduler.html)). The useful exception for a PC comes from hybrid CPU/GPU MoE engines. There, "scheduling" moves *inside one token*. KTransformers folds CPU expert work into the same CUDA graph through `cudaLaunchHostFunc` callbacks (up to 1.23x decode) and adds Expert Deferral (up to 1.45x more, with an average accuracy drop of at most 0.5%). With Expert Deferral, low-scoring experts in layer k contribute only at layer k+2, through the residual stream, and CPU utilization rises from under 75% to ~100% ([KTransformers SOSP'25](https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf)). The OSDI'26 paper on local MoE applies intra-node P/D with zero-copy shared weights, but the +50% throughput gain appears **only under concurrency** ([arXiv 2606.10493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10493)). Single-node KV tiering, by contrast, has a large effect on TTFT on a cache hit: **2.2-32.8x versus recomputation** in an NVMe study ([arXiv 2609.11744](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11744)).

## For pancake-strata, disaggregation moves inside one token

The machine profile decides everything. An RX 7900 XT (detected and working now; the older note saying otherwise is outdated) decodes ~126 tok/s exact on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (125B MoE, 512 experts top-10, hybrid DeltaNet, MTP), with a Ryzen 7 5700G (8 Zen3 cores, AVX2) and PCIe 3.0. A decode window is **~64% GPU work, ~21% CPU experts and ~9% MTP drafting**. With one request in flight, every cluster idea that arbitrates between requests goes to zero. Three targets remain: the CPU share on the critical path, drafting, and the TTFT of agent turns. The theoretical limit for fully hiding the CPU work is roughly 1/(1−0.21) ≈ 1.27x, i.e. ~160 tok/s. That is my own estimate, not measured, and it assumes all of the CPU share is on the critical path.

### What the engine already has

Most of the foundations are already in place. The verify window is captured as a single HIP graph, with CPU experts overlapped. That is the equivalent of KTransformers' host-callback trick, so that 1.23x is probably already banked. The scheduler is in C++, and the Python server talks to it over a pipe, Ollama-style. At ~8 ms/token, one pipe hop per token is cheap if streaming is asynchronous. The CPU/PCIe split for missed experts (`--pcie-frac 0`, measured best on PCIe 3.0: 111.7→118.4 and 92.5→104.3 tok/s) is the same idea llama.cpp brought into mainline only in November 2026 as "CPU share" (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md`). The conversation cache that parks KV in RAM and on disk is the single-PC version of LMCache/Mooncake tiering. The message-boundary checkpoint, which saves DeltaNet state, cut the first edit from 17.2 s to 11.5 s (−33%) (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/docs/MESSAGE_BOUNDARY_CACHE.md`). This is a real differentiator: prefix reuse for Qwen3-Next in llama.cpp was still reported broken ([llama.cpp #18497](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/18497)). KV prefetch during prefill gives −5.3% at 32K (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/docs/KV_PREFETCH.md`).

### What to adopt, in order of expected gain

**1. A tuned Expert Deferral, as a lossy opt-in flag.** It is the only published mechanism that targets exactly the ~21% of CPU work on the critical path between layers. The idea is to defer to layer k+2 only the cache-missed experts with the lowest weight in the top-10, so CPU work overlaps the next layer's attention and DeltaNet on the GPU. The local status is not encouraging: Strata's light variant (`STRATA_VERIFY_DEFER_CPU`) gave +8% on short replies and −7% on code editing, with changed output (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/docs/PANCAKE_FINDINGS.md`). On HIP it still produces non-finite logits with the ported RDNA3 kernels (`/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md`). The first step is fixing the HIP bug, then measuring KL, task accuracy and the effect on MTP acceptance. KTransformers' accuracy data comes from DeepSeek-V3-class models with 8 routed experts, not from top-10 of 512.

**2. MTP drafting in the same graph and overlapped with the CPU tail.** Drafting takes ~9% of the window. llama.cpp found graph allocation to be the main bottleneck in its MTP work ([F1LM1/llama.cpp PR #4](https://github.meowingcats01.workers.dev/F1LM1/llama.cpp/pull/4)), and re-enabling CUDA graphs on MoE gave +5% at batch 1 ([TurinTech](https://www.turintech.ai/blog/inside-llama-cpp-pr-26802)). The gain is exact, lossless, and comes from capturing the draft step together with verification and from avoiding recapture when the window shape changes.

**3. A cache policy aware of agent turns.** No new tier is needed, just better rules: an anticipatory checkpoint at every message boundary, longest-prefix matching across parked conversations (system prompt and tool schema, shared across sessions), and keeping DeltaNet snapshots where they will be reused. A full per-token radix tree does not help, because the DeltaNet layers have state only at checkpoint positions.

**4. A thinking budget with a transition phrase.** It is the cheapest win from the "runtime intelligence" family. The server watches the `<think>` span and injects `</think>` plus a transition phrase at the cap, following vLLM's `thinking_token_budget` ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/reasoning_outputs.html)). Qwen tolerates truncation by design ([Qwen3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.09388)). It does not raise tok/s, but it cuts total time per reply. Dynasor-CoT-style probing is worth it only for verifiable answers (math, multiple choice) and requires a sequence fork with a shared KV prefix ([Dynasor](https://github.com/hao-ai-lab/Dynasor)).

**5. Weight streaming in prefill, only for TTFT at 32K+.** Stream-loading experts to the GPU for large ubatches is the main prefill lever for local MoE: 1,200 tok/s on DeepSeek-V3 ([arXiv 2606.10493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10493)). On PCIe 3.0 (~12 GB/s) the ceiling is lower. It is worth checking whether pancake's prefill already sends experts to the GPU in large ubatches instead of computing misses on the 8 cores.

### What does not apply and why

| Idea | Why not |
|---|---|
| Physical P/D disaggregation (llm-d, Dynamo, Mooncake, DistServe) | One GPU, so there is nowhere to send the KV. One user, so there is no concurrent decode to protect. Strata's two-GPU version lost 4-48%. |
| Chunked-prefill interleaving | It only protects the TPOT of *other* requests. Chunk size remains a TTFT knob (32K proved better than 16K together with boundary checkpoints). |
| SGLang/TRT-LLM-style overlap scheduler | Already done in spirit: HIP graph plus C++ scheduler. The extra decode step conflicts with speculative verification. All that remains is to make sure the server never blocks the engine (estimated gain: a few percent, not measured). |
| Compile-time typed FSM (TokenSpeed) | It solves correctness of concurrency across many requests and shared KV blocks. With one request and an existing C++ engine, it brings no speed. |
| Service-engine decoupling for heterogeneous accelerators (xLLM, vLLM plugins) | A single accelerator type (gfx1100). The Vega iGPU is Vulkan-only and adds no useful bandwidth. |
| Distributed KV store (Mooncake Store, Redis, remote LMCache) | On a single PC, local RAM and disk already are the tiers. A network layer only adds latency. |
| Batch slots for parallel sub-agents | Helps only if you actually run concurrent agents. Each slot takes VRAM from the expert cache (11-24% on a 12 GB card, `/home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/docs/BATCHING.md`). |
| Reasoning-aware scheduling (PASCAL, Dynasor as a scheduler) | It reorders phases *between* concurrent requests. With one request there is nothing to reorder ([PASCAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11530v1)). |
| KV compression for reasoning (R-KV, ThinKV, TIP) | Lossy or requires a specially trained model. Sparse attention and DeltaNet already bound KV growth. It would matter only for thinking traces of 128K+. |
| Exo / self-editing policies | They belong to the agent layer (harnesses like opencode), not the engine. |

## Conclusion

The lesson of 2026 is not that prefill and decode must be split, but that **separation makes sense exactly where two things compete for the same resource**. In a cluster, requests compete for GPUs, hence P/D, prefix routing and flow control. Inside a single pancake process, the competition is between the GPU and the 8 Zen3 cores within each MoE layer, so the local equivalent of disaggregation is loosening the dependencies between layers (Expert Deferral), not moving KV between machines. The most valuable part of cluster architectures for this engine is not a scheduler but cache discipline: prefix routing and KV tiering deliver a large share of the gains llm-d reports even without P/D, and pancake already has the local variant, which is technically harder because of DeltaNet's recurrent state.

For AI-written overviews, the practical rule is to check the name, the mechanism and the number separately. The names all passed. The mechanisms were generally right. The numbers and guarantees were inflated or taken out of context. Every "up to" has to be traced to its full range and to the system layer it refers to. Otherwise, a cost saving at the agent level (Exo) gets read as an inference speedup.
