# Berkeley and CMU give pancake two decisive measurements

Among US universities outside Stanford, MIT and Harvard, **two Berkeley-lead papers directly hit pancake's real bottleneck**, that is, the bytes the CPU reads for missed experts. The first is **activation sparsity inside the experts** (Park et al., 2026), with 84.5-87.4% sparsity on the Qwen3.5 MoEs at 95% of the average score. The second is **FreeToken** (Shuo Yang et al., 2026), the first open-source engine for hybrid GDN MoE on consumer hardware, with recurrent state checkpoints at thinking and tool-call boundaries. Of the three sparsity variants for the CPU experts (CATS from Stanford, TEAL from MIT, the gate mask from Berkeley), **the Berkeley variant is tested first**. It is the only one with evidence on MoE and on Qwen, and it saves both up and down rows. The test is an offline measurement, a single pass with all three masks, verified by token forcing. The second important result is methodological and comes from CMU (Baleen, S3-FIFO) and Berkeley (ADRS/OpenEvolve). The gap between the pancake tier (79.6%), LRU (89.5%) and Belady (94.7%) is a gap in **hit rate**, not in **time**. On PCIe 3.0, a promotion costs about three times as much as computing a miss on the CPU. So first compute a Belady ceiling that includes the promotion cost, and only then search for policies. An unexpected result: **pancake already has most of what the drafting papers propose** (SuffixDrafter, DraftPolicy with measured cost for every window size, the MTP vs. lookup choice at every step). All that is left to add is a suffix index shared across sessions (SuffixDecoding, CMU) and an audit against ReplaySSM. The FreeToken miss split seems to be exactly `--pcie-frac`, already measured best at 0, so it must be checked before any code. On the agent side, the best numbers are: AsyncFC (Berkeley, 1.4x end-to-end on code), Slipstream (Princeton, −39.7% latency through asynchronous compaction), Interruptible LRMs (graceful stopping of thinking) and GEPA, which replaces MIPROv2 from the Stanford plan. All numbers are the authors', obtained on other hardware. Nothing below has been measured on our machine yet.

## How I read the papers: what pancake already has and what failed

**Lead** means the first author or the senior group is at the named institution. **Co** means the institution appears only among the co-authors. **Inferred** means the affiliation comes from known lab membership (Sky Lab, SqueezeAILab, the Netravali group, the Abdelfattah lab) and was not read in the PDF. The report covers Berkeley, CMU, UW, Princeton, Cornell, UCSD, Duke, Rice, Stevens, Ohio State, UT Austin, Yale and Emory, for the period Nov. 2022 - Nov. 2026. Papers already discussed in the Stanford and MIT/Harvard reports (SGLang, Hydragen, FlexGen, Quest, DuoAttention, TEAL, Hydra, Gated DeltaNet) appear only as points of comparison.

The verdicts start from pancake's measured profile on the RX 7900 XT. In decode, the GPU works **15.9 ms out of a 24.8 ms window**, and the rest of the window is dominated by the experts computed on the CPU, at ~33 GB/s, close to the dual-channel DDR4 limit. Decode reached ~112-121 tok/s after porting the RDNA3 kernels, with `--pcie-frac 0` and an adaptive tier at every window ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). An IQ2_XS expert is ~1.4 MB, and the 5700G → GPU link is PCIe 3.0. Pancake already has: a tiered expert cache with an adaptive tier, miss computation on the CPU, a PCIe share (`--pcie-frac`, best at 0), MTP + `SuffixDrafter` (trigram index over the prompt and the accepted output, up to 512k tokens), `DraftPolicy` (measured cost for every window size, acceptance rate per match-length bucket, the MTP vs. lookup choice at every round), GDN state replay, grammar inside speculation, a parked conversation cache with checkpoints at message boundaries, `reasoning_budget_by_effort`, tool-call recovery and the loop guard being ported from Project Maya. **Already tested without gain**: HIP graphs, unroll, Vulkan, TurboQuant KV, L3 prefetch (`STRATA_ARENA_WARM`), ClassLRU scoring, "reuse-aware" admission (failed because of the PCIe 3.0 cost), DraftExpert-style miss-aware windows and the GDN megakernel in decode (0.0 ms, because the window waits for the CPU rows). Nothing on this list is recommended again without a new reason.

This gives the selection criterion. A paper matters for decode only if it **reduces the bytes read by the CPU**, **raises the useful time of the hit rate** (not just the rate itself) or **reduces the number of generated tokens**. At the level of an agent turn, prefill avoided also matters, as does the dead time in which the GPU waits for tools.

## The Berkeley gate mask goes ahead of TEAL and CATS

The Stanford report proposed CATS (threshold on |SiLU(gate)|, up/down computed only for the active neurons). The MIT report replaced it with TEAL, because **CATS degenerates at 40% sparsity on Llama-3-8B (PPL ~7.6e4)**, while TEAL holds 6.21 versus 5.87 dense ([arXiv 2408.14690](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)). The Berkeley paper changes the order again. **Uncovering Intra-expert Activation Sparsity for Efficient MoE Model Execution** (Jongseok Park, Sunga Kim, Zhenyu Gu, Ion Stoica, Alvin Cheung; Berkeley Sky lead, May 2026) shows that pre-trained MoEs have up to 90% sparsity inside each expert, with no change to the model ([arXiv 2605.08575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08575)). The mechanism is in fact the one from CATS, applied to experts. The gate is computed densely, neurons with |activation| below the threshold are masked, and up and down are computed only for the gathered neurons. The threshold comes from a sparsity → threshold table built at engine start, and the shared experts stay fully active. On the models closest to ours, the threshold that keeps 95% of the benchmark average is **84.5% (Qwen3.5-35B-A3B) and 87.4% (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B)**. On Granite-1B, the same threshold drops to 26.5% ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08575)). The paper measures only the GPU (2.5x on the MoE layer on an RTX 4090, 1.2x end-to-end in vLLM), and the gain is smallest at small batch.

The contradiction between CATS and Berkeley has an explanation. CATS failed on dense MLPs, and fine-grained MoE experts are, according to Berkeley, much sparser, not less sparse, as both earlier reports feared. Two warnings remain. First: "95% of the score" is a lossy criterion. On Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the average drops from **70.9 to 67.5** at 85% sparsity. For a coding agent on IQ2_XS, already fragile according to Scaling Laws for Precision (Harvard), this is a real loss, not noise. Second: nobody has tested sparsity at 2-3 bits, nor on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next.

The Berkeley mask wins on bytes, not only on evidence. With the gate mask, the CPU reads the whole gate (~1/3 of the expert) and only the active fraction of up and down: **0.33 + 0.67·(1−s)**. With TEAL on the down input, the criterion is more precise, because it is the actual input of down, but up must be computed in full, so it reads **0.67 + 0.33·(1−s)**. At 85%, the Berkeley variant reads ~43% of the bytes, and TEAL ~72%. In our window, the non-GPU part is at most ~8.9 ms. If CPU time drops in proportion to bytes, the Berkeley mask at 85% saves ~5 ms out of 24.8, that is a **ceiling of ~1.25x on decode**. At 70%, the ceiling is ~1.2x. Realistically, after the gather overhead and quality margins, the gain would be **10-20%**. My estimate is lower than the one in the Berkeley notes (1.4x), because those assumed the CPU takes half of decode, not the measured part above the 15.9 ms GPU floor.

The test is done in a single offline pass. On replayed opencode traces, three masks are collected per layer and per expert on the same FP32 forward: |gate| (Berkeley), |SiLU(gate)| with a global threshold (CATS) and |SiLU(gate)·up| on the down input (TEAL, as a quality reference). Activations are zeroed in simulation at 50/70/85%, and top-token mismatch and KL are measured with the existing token-forcing harness, against the stock noise of 1.17-1.30%. The AVX2 kernel is written only if the gate mask passes the gate at ≥70%. Skipping up rows is natural in GGUF, because the blocks run along the hidden dimension. Down needs an n_ff-major re-pack at load time, which means transposed requantization of a tensor that is already IQ2_XS, an extra quality risk that must be measured separately. The re-pack is the same one CATS and TEAL required, so the code is not lost, whichever criterion wins.

## The expert cache ceiling is measured in milliseconds, not in hit percentages

The "other US" notes read the gap 79.6% (tier) < 89.5% (per-layer LRU) < 94.7% (Belady), measured at 10,909 cached experts (44%), as a policy problem that recency would solve. Their recommendation is a dynamic SIEVE segment next to the frequency tier. **SIEVE** (Emory lead, CMU co; NSDI 2024) means FIFO plus a "visited" bit and a moving hand, with a miss ratio up to 63.2% lower than ARC, but only ~1.5% on average ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi24/presentation/zhang-yazhuo)). **S3-FIFO** (CMU lead; SOSP 2023) adds a small probation FIFO, which filters out objects used only once before they reach the main cache ([Yang](https://juncheng.seas.harvard.edu)). Both papers, however, measure miss ratio, not time, and this is where the analogy breaks.

The pancake data already show it. "Reuse-aware" admission failed because of the PCIe 3.0 cost, and the per-window tier with 160 swaps raised the rate only to 85.0%, yet it gave +16% on decode. A simple calculation explains why. A 1.4 MB expert goes up over PCIe 3.0 (~12 GB/s) in ~115 µs, but is computed on the CPU at ~33 GB/s in ~42 µs. **A promotion costs about as much as three CPU computations of the same expert**, and the DMA transfer reads from the same DDR4 that the CPU already saturates. The LRU replay that gives 89.5% counts every miss as a free load, so the real ceiling is unknown.

The coherent recommendation has three steps. **First, the Baleen method** (CMU lead, FAST 2024): the ceiling of the best possible policy is computed offline before building one ([USENIX FAST'24](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast24/presentation/wong)). Concretely, the existing replay simulator is extended with a time model: CPU cost per miss, PCIe promotion cost (including DDR contention) and the GPU slack in which a promotion can hide. Then simulated decode is computed for the tier, for LRU with promotion on every miss and for a Belady with "bypass", which promotes only when the next reuse comes soon enough to amortize the ~115 µs. If Belady with bypass beats the tier by less than ~5% of decode time, work on eviction policies stops here. **The second step**, only if the ceiling justifies it: a dynamic SIEVE/S3-FIFO segment, in which a VRAM slot is earned by a second hit in the probation queue. This admission resembles the "reuse-aware" admission that already failed. The difference is that here the promotion cost enters the rule explicitly. **The third step**, ADRS-style policy search. "Barbarians at the Gate" (Berkeley lead) shows that OpenEvolve can evolve a policy on a replayable simulator in ~5 h and for under $10. For expert balancing in parallelism, it found a 3.7 ms policy versus ~540 ms for DeepSeek EPLB ([arXiv 2510.06189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06189)). The paper has internally inconsistent numbers (EPLB 2x in the table, 5.0x in the text) and publishes no code, so it is a method, not a result. For pancake, the simulated objective must be decode time, not hit rate, and the winners are validated on the machine. A cheaper variant of the same idea, suggested by the notes without a source: a per-expert "cache-friendly / averse" classifier, trained on our own Belady replays, following the Hawkeye/Glider principle (UT Austin, from before the window).

Expert predictors from the US literature stay in second place. **fMoE/FineMoE** (Stevens lead; EuroSys '26) matches the prompt embedding against historical "expert maps", with −47% latency and +36% hit rate in v1 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05370v1)). **ExFlow** (Ohio State lead; IPDPS 2024) shows strong expert affinity between consecutive layers, estimable from ~5,000 tokens ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.08383v2)). **SiDA-MoE** (Duke lead; MLSys 2024) trains an LSTM predictor with up to 99% accuracy ([MLSys](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/698cfaf72a208aef2e78bcac55b74328-Abstract-Conference.html)). **eMoE** (UVA affiliation unconfirmed) and **Read-ME** (UT Austin lead; NeurIPS 2024) complete the family, but Read-ME needs model retraining ([NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/94027)). At ~165 µs per layer at 126 t/s, the PCIe slack allows about one prefetched expert per layer and per token, so precision matters more than recall. The failure of the DraftExpert windows and of the L3 prefetch warns that prediction-driven loads may not pay off here. The only cheap piece is the one from fMoE: at the start of a conversation, the expert profile is chosen by the nearest prompt embedding, not by the domain label. This only speeds up warm-up.

**The FreeToken split must be treated as "check first".** FreeToken copies over PCIe q* ≈ m·B_P/B_H of the m missed experts of a layer and runs them on the GPU, while the rest run concurrently on the CPU, with the CPU branch launched first ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16157)). At B_P ≈ 12 and B_H ≈ 33 GB/s, this gives q* ≈ 0.36m. The same split already exists, though: MOD_PLAN notes that the PCIe share in Strata is "the same split" as the CPU share in llama.cpp PR #30966, that **`--pcie-frac 0` was measured best (+6/+13%)**, and that `STRATA_PARTIAL_PIN` (the GPU computes part of the misses over PCIe) came out "no faster" ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). If reading the code confirms that the pancake split is per step and concurrent with the CPU, then the local measurement has already refuted q* on this machine, most likely through DDR4 contention. The only possible difference is that FreeToken inserts the copied experts into the global LRU cache (promotion) instead of using them transiently. This is checked in the same code read. For multiple tokens per expert (verification with k drafts, prefill), **Fiddler** (UW lead; ICLR 2025) proposes that the CPU vs. transfer choice depend on the number of tokens that reach the expert ([arXiv 2402.07033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07033)). Pancake already has `STRATA_PREFILL_CPU_SHARE`, so here too it must be checked whether the threshold depends on the number of tokens or is fixed.

## Pancake's drafting is already ahead of most papers

Four papers seem to propose new things, but three of them are already in the code. **Speculative Decoding: Performance or Illusion?** (Berkeley lead) is the first study inside a production engine. Verification takes 42-95% of the time. A single MTP head reused autoregressively has a falling per-position acceptance, **0.92 → 0.68 → 0.38**. N-gram drafts beat EAGLE when prompt/output overlap is high (code editing), and an oracle that picks per position between n-gram and EAGLE would give up to 2.2x over the best fixed strategy ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11580)). **TurboSpec** (Berkeley lead) tunes the amount of speculation online by goodput ([arXiv 2406.14066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14066)). **Sequoia** (CMU lead) picks the shape and size of the draft tree by measured hardware cost ([arXiv 2402.12374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12374v2)). Pancake's `DraftPolicy` already does all three for chains: `cost_ms(t)` is the measured round time for each window size (the RTX 5070 curve is only a prior), lookup acceptance is learned per match-length bucket, and `choose()` compares at every round the expected tokens per millisecond for MTP and for lookup. That is exactly the "expert union cost" the CMU notes ask for. What is left from the MTP decay curve is a check: the table that `DraftPolicy` learns for MTP must show the same drop after position 2-3. If it does not, MTP depth is capped.

**SuffixDecoding** (CMU lead with Snowflake; NeurIPS 2025 spotlight) is the only paper that brings something new. Suffix trees index prompts and **previous outputs**, and the speculation length adapts by frequency scores, with up to 3.9x on SWE-Bench and ~2.2x over EAGLE-2/3 ([arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04975v3)). Pancake's `SuffixDrafter` already covers prompts and accepted output, but for a single sequence: it keeps only the 4 most recent positions per trigram and picks the longest match. The real differences from the paper are two. First: an index **shared across sessions**, that is, parked conversations, sub-agents and earlier sessions on the same repository, where tool-call JSON, diffs and boilerplate repeat. Second: **frequency scoring** of continuations, not recency. Both can be evaluated offline with `suffix_drafter_test --simulate` on opencode traces, before any engine change. The estimated gain is a few percent up to ~10% on edit turns, on top of the A/B already scheduled with `--lookup-max-t 12/16`. Draft trees stay a bad idea here: each branch needs either a copy of the GDN state or a recompute, and adds new experts to the verify window.

**Lookahead Decoding** (UCSD lead, Berkeley co; ~1.8x on MT-Bench) ([arXiv 2402.02057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02057)), **CLLMs** (UCSD/SJTU) and **Jacobi Forcing** (UCSD lead; 3.8-4.0x on code, but with fine-tuning) ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14681)) do not apply. Wide guesses multiply the expert union, and the strong variants need training the 125B model. **Medusa** (Princeton lead; ICML 2024) is dominated by the native MTP ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10774v3)). All that is left from it is "typical acceptance", as an opt-in mode only for thinking tokens, with an estimated gain of 5-15% there and a risk of reasoning drift. SparseSpec, QuantSpec, TriForce, MagicDec, MagicPIG and TidalDecode attack a KV/attention cost that is not our bottleneck.

**ReplaySSM** (Dao AI Lab, Princeton, with NVIDIA; 2026 blog and public repo) is the only recurrent state paper worth an audit. For GDN, instead of load/update/store at every step, u = β(v − S·k) is kept in a ring buffer. Rejected drafts are undone by moving a pointer, and verification uses a single T×T triangular solve. The reported result is 1.87-1.96x on speculation in batched vLLM ([Tri Dao blog](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/replayssm/)). For us, GDN state traffic is on the order of 2-3% of GPU bandwidth, so plain decode would gain 1-3%. Speculation would gain 0-8%, but only if pancake's GDN replay today copies a snapshot per draft token or re-runs the recurrence serially on rollback. The question is settled by reading the replay code, before any port. **The Mamba in the Llama** (Cornell lead; NeurIPS 2024) is superseded by ReplaySSM ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.15237v4)).

## Checkpoints at tool boundaries give the cheapest per-turn gain

Four papers converge on the parked conversation cache. **Marconi** (Princeton lead, AWS co; MLSys 2025) is the prefix cache policy designed for hybrids ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19379)). The SSM state is saved at the branch points of the radix tree and always at the last decoded token. Eviction uses the score **recency + α·(FLOPs saved / state bytes)**, with α tuned by replay. On SWE-bench traces, the token hit rate is **34.4x versus vLLM+**, and TTFT drops by up to 71.1% ([HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.19379v3)). **FreeToken** puts recurrent state checkpoints at thinking, tool-call, tool-output and turn boundaries. After a context edit, the engine restores the deepest checkpoint that survived ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16157)). **InferCept** (UCSD lead; ICML 2024) chooses between Discard, Preserve and Swap when a request stops for a tool ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01869)). With a single user, the answer is always Preserve. **Continuum** (Berkeley lead) keeps the KV pinned with a TTL after a tool call ([arXiv 2511.02230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02230)), and **MORI** (Berkeley lead) orders sessions by "idleness", with 20-71% more throughput on Claude Code traces ([arXiv 2606.00866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00866)).

Pancake already has checkpoints at message boundaries, so the new part is small and cheap: extra triggers at the end of the tool call and of the tool result, exactly where opencode edits the context (compaction, trimming tool outputs). One warning the notes do not give: Qwen templates usually strip `reasoning_content` from earlier turns. A checkpoint after `</think>` is valid only if the next prompt keeps the thinking text, which must be checked on the real template before adding that trigger. Marconi's FLOP/byte score can replace pure LRU between the VRAM/RAM/NVMe tiers of the conversation cache. The TTL/idleness idea from Continuum and MORI matters only for switches between sub-agents. All of this extends Stanford action #5 (cache with several prompt states and GDN snapshots). The gain is seconds per edited turn, not tokens per second. **Prompt Cache** (Yale lead; MLSys 2024) does not transfer: modular, non-prefix reuse is impossible for GDN layers, whose state depends on the whole prefix ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04934)).

The idea with the most potential here does not appear explicitly in any paper. It is **KVFlow applied to experts**. KVFlow (UCSD lead; NeurIPS 2025) prefetches the KV of the next step from a graph of agent steps, with 1.83x versus SGLang's hierarchical radix cache ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07400v1)). In opencode, the next step is predictable: a tool result of a known type follows (file read, grep, test output). The GPU and PCIe sit idle anyway while the tool runs. The 160-swaps-per-window mechanism already exists, so the VRAM tier can be reshaped for the expert set of the expected prefill, before the tool output arrives. The risk is the one already seen with DraftExpert and the L3 prefetch: prediction may not pay off. That is why the idea first goes through the time-cost simulator from the previous section.

## The agent gains from asynchrony, not from kernels

The best per-turn speed numbers come from overlapping the model with the tools. **AsyncFC** (Berkeley lead) keeps decoding while functions run, using symbolic "futures", and runs independent calls in parallel, with no fine-tuning. The result: **1.4x end-to-end on coding tasks** at the same accuracy ([arXiv 2605.15077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15077)). For us, this means an opencode client fork, with medium-high effort. Pancake is idle during tools anyway. **Slipstream** (Princeton lead, inferred; the Netravali group, as with Marconi) runs compaction asynchronously. A judge checks the summary against the agent's next steps before swapping it in: **+8.8 pp accuracy and −39.7% latency** on SWE-bench Verified, with Qwen3.5-9B ([arXiv 2605.08580](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08580)). On a single GPU, compaction is scheduled in the tool windows, and a Marconi checkpoint is placed at the compaction boundary. The idea complements ACE (Stanford #6, delta compaction) and RLM (MIT #8, externalizing bulky data). Slipstream solves the timing of compaction, the other two solve its content. **LLMCompiler** (Berkeley SqueezeAILab; up to 3.7x lower latency than ReAct) ([arXiv 2312.04511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04511)) comes down, for us, to prompting for reads and greps grouped in the same message, plus checking that tool-call recovery supports several calls per turn.

On thinking, Berkeley strengthens the Stanford direction. **The Danger of Overthinking** (Berkeley lead) finds on 4,018 SWE-Bench trajectories that excessive thinking correlates with lower success. Picking the solution with less thinking raises performance by almost 30% and cuts 43% of compute ([arXiv 2502.08235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08235)). **Reasoning Models Can Be Effective Without Thinking** (Berkeley lead) shows that, at equal budget, NoThinking beats Thinking (51.3 vs. 28.9 at 700 tokens) ([arXiv 2504.09858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09858)). Both support Stanford #2: default effort `low`/`medium`, with escalation on failure. **Are Large Reasoning Models Interruptible?** (Berkeley lead) adds exactly what the cap lacks: under budget cuts, accuracy drops by up to 60%, through "reasoning leakage", "panic" and "self-doubt" ([arXiv 2510.11713](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11713)). Concretely, when `reasoning_budget_by_effort` hits the cap, a short closing hint is injected instead of an abrupt `</think>`, and we monitor whether answers start to contain reasoning. This refines Stanford #3 and combines with the stop probe from MIT #3. **Kinetics** (CMU lead) argues that long thinking becomes attention-bound on pure transformers ([arXiv 2506.05333](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05333v3)). On our hybrid, the per-token cost grows more slowly, so a longer budget is cheaper than best-of-n.

On reliability, three ideas are cheap. **SWE-agent** (Princeton lead) introduced the agent-computer interface: windowed file viewing, summarized search and edits rejected by a linter if syntactically invalid ([arXiv 2405.15793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15793)). **mini-SWE-agent** shows that strong models manage with bash alone, which supports a small tool set for a local 2-3 bit model ([SWE-bench Bash Only](https://www.swebench.com/bash-only.html)). **Agent Workflow Memory** (CMU lead) induces reusable workflows from successful trajectories, with +51.1% relative on WebArena ([arXiv 2409.07429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07429)), and is the direct relative of Dynamic Cheatsheet from the Stanford report. **GEPA** (Berkeley lead, with Stanford/Databricks co) beats MIPROv2 by over 10% and GRPO by 6% on average, using up to 35 times fewer rollouts ([arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)). Therefore **GEPA replaces MIPROv2 in Stanford #13** for the compaction prompt and the tool-call format, run at night, with pancake as the reflection LM. **XGrammar-2** (CMU lead; CAIS '26) brings TagDispatch: free text up to a tag, then the grammar of that tool, with the masks computed on the CPU ([arXiv 2601.04426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04426v2)). Pancake already has grammar in its speculation, so XGrammar-2 is worth it only if the matcher shows up in the decode profile. For Avalon (GBNF from llama.cpp), the relative gain is probably larger.

## Measurement: ceilings before policies, distributions instead of numbers

The series already has a good gate: token forcing with mismatch against stock noise, then Terminal-Bench (Stanford #1) and compute-quality curves (MIT #11). US universities add four tools. **BFCL v4** (Berkeley lead), with the multi-turn subset, tests tool-call format and arguments, and **LiveCodeBench** (Berkeley lead) is uncontaminated ([leaderboard](https://gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/leaderboard.html), [arXiv 2403.07974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974)). Both are gates before changing the quantization level, enabling sparsity or changing tool-call recovery. I have not checked whether the BFCL runner accepts a local OpenAI-compatible endpoint. **HELMET** (Princeton lead) shows that synthetic NIAH tests do not predict real performance and recommends RAG and re-ranking tasks for fast iteration ([project](https://princeton-nlp.github.io/HELMET)). Any requantization or cache change at 32-128k is checked on these subsets, not on NIAH. **TheAgentCompany** (CMU lead) brings checkpoint-based grading with partial credit ([arXiv 2412.14161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14161v2)). Only the grading is reused, on a small set of tasks from our own repositories, not the heavy environment with GitLab and RocketChat. **The Price Reversal Phenomenon** (Stanford lead, Berkeley co) finds variations of up to 9.7x in thinking tokens between identical runs ([arXiv 2603.23971](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23971)). Any effort or budget A/B must therefore be run repeatedly and reported as a distribution.

The Baleen method is also a measurement tool: exported routing traces, a tier simulator with time costs, a ceiling before a policy. The same trace infrastructure then serves three actions (the cache ceiling, the ADRS search, the shared suffix index simulation), so it is built once. The Berkeley SD study gives the last criterion: speculation is measured in the engine, as the percentage of time spent in verification, not just as acceptance.

The order has a precise logic. P1 and P3 are one-to-two-day measurements that decide the two biggest decode bets in the whole portfolio. P5 and P9 are code reads that can close two ideas without writing a line. P6 is the cheapest per-turn gain. On the agent side, A1 and A2 do not touch the engine, and A3 and A4 attack the GPU's dead time while tools run. Explicitly skipped, for the reasons in the sections above: draft trees, Lookahead/CLLMs/Jacobi Forcing, SparseSpec/QuantSpec, TriForce/MagicDec/MagicPIG/TidalDecode, KVQuant/XQuant, requantization to IQ*_KT, FlashInfer, Mirage MPK, NanoFlow/Punica/Atom, NoMAD-Attention, DFloat11, Read-ME, SiDA, non-prefix Prompt Cache and the multi-tenant systems (NEO, Pie, Jenga, Autellix).

A few gaps remain for the next reports. No US paper evaluates expert offload on a consumer AMD GPU, on DDR4 bandwidth or on a recurrent hybrid at batch 1, and none reports the hit rate against Belady for a model with ~500 experts per layer. The code for the Berkeley sparsity paper was not found (it is "in appendix B"), and the affiliations of Slipstream, TokenButler/xKV and eMoE are inferred. The literature on expert caching and prediction at batch 1 (MoE-Infinity, HOBBIT, Pre-gated MoE, ExpertFlow, PreScope) is European and Asian, so it belongs to the Europe and Asia reports.

## Conclusion

The series started with the question "which paper makes pancake faster" and arrives at a more precise one: which paper reduces the bytes read by the CPU without moving the cost onto PCIe. Stanford answered with CATS, MIT with TEAL, and Berkeley brings the first evidence that real MoE experts, including Qwen, are much sparser than the dense MLPs on which CATS failed. The paradox is that the winning mask is still the one from CATS, but applied where the sparsity exists. On the cache, the CMU lesson is that a hit rate is not a time. On a PCIe 3.0 machine, where a promotion costs as much as three CPU computations, the 15-point gap to Belady may be worth much less than it looks. A ceiling computed with real costs prevents a third round of failed policies, after ClassLRU and reuse-aware admission.

The second finding concerns pancake's maturity. SuffixDrafter, DraftPolicy with measured cost, the GDN replay and the checkpoints at message boundaries already cover what SuffixDecoding, TurboSpec, Sequoia and half of FreeToken and Marconi propose. The remaining margin is narrow and lies in the details: a cross-session index, a checkpoint trigger at the tool boundary, a u buffer instead of snapshots. The biggest untapped reserve is not in the engine. It is the time in which the GPU waits for tools: AsyncFC, Slipstream and KVFlow-style expert pre-staging turn it from dead time into useful time, and this has not been tried on this machine yet.
