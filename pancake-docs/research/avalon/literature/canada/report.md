# Canada strengthens the agent, not the pancake engine

The most useful Canadian papers for the project do not speed up decoding. They change **how we measure** and **how we stop an agent that goes off the rails**. First place goes to **"Displacement Is Not Direction" (Toronto lead, Moshovos lab)**. The paper shows that per-token KLD, perplexity and even top-1 agreement no longer predict task scores when quantizations are close to the reference. The measurement was done on exactly the model families we run (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Devstral). The direct consequence: token forcing in pancake's ladder of quality gates can filter out bad variants, but it cannot decide between good ones. Second place goes to three sources on agent behavior: **UBC/Queen's** (failed runs cost 1.4 to 4.4 times more tokens, and agents "do not fail fast"), **Thoughtology (Mila/McGill lead)**, which measures rumination through 5-gram repetition, and **ECLoop (Concordia lead)**, which adds +4.8 to +11.8 pp on SWE-bench Verified with up to 12.1% fewer tokens. From them comes a concrete and cheap extension of the Maya loop guard, which is already on by default in pancake. On the engine side, Canada brings little. **SpAx (Toronto lead)** becomes the fourth mask in the item 13 sparsity study, not a new item. **Cactus (Alberta lead)** does nothing at greedy decoding and almost nothing at T=0.1, the temperature the user chose explicitly, so it stays parked. For expert offload, caching or prediction I found no Canadian paper. The round is smaller than those for the US, Europe and Asia, and the report is sized to match.

## How I read the papers: what transfers to batch 1 and to the agent

**Lead** means the first author or the senior group is at the named Canadian institution. **Co** means the institution appears only among the co-authors. Coverage includes Mila/UdeM, McGill, Polytechnique, Concordia, Toronto/Vector, Waterloo, Alberta/Amii, UBC and Queen's. The researchers' notes systematically listed arXiv papers from Nov. 2022 - Nov. 2026 for about 25 faculty in Montreal and Toronto. For the rest of Canada they used web searches. Coverage for SFU, McMaster, Ottawa, Calgary and Western is weak, so the absence of results from there is weak evidence, not a certainty.

The criterion is the same as in the previous reports. At decode, the GPU works **15.9 ms of a 24.8 ms window**, and the rest of the window is set by the experts computed on the CPU. Replay at 44% of experts in VRAM gives **79.6% hit for the engine tier, 89.5% for LRU and 94.7% for Belady** ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). A paper counts if it reduces the bytes read by the CPU, hides CPU time under GPU work, reduces tokens wasted by the agent, or makes quantization and sparsity decisions safer. Pancake already has almost everything the Canadian drafting papers propose: `DraftPolicy` (adaptive length and the choice of MTP vs suffix), `--spec-min-p 0.5` (chain stopped at low confidence, EAGLE-2 style) and DISCO, ported today on `feat/disco`. It also has per-level thinking budgets, soft close and a conversation cache with GDN checkpoints. I excluded papers led by companies (Microsoft Research Montréal, ServiceNow, Borealis), except SWE-Effi, kept as a metric source.

## Quality gates need tasks, not KLD

The Moshovos paper ([arXiv 2606.19558](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19558)) evaluated **28 quantizations of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and 41 of Devstral-Small-2-24B**. Across the whole cohort, KLD correlates well with the benchmark score: **ρ = -0.72** on Qwen and **-0.86** on Devstral. In the "quiet zone", close to the reference, the correlation disappears: **ρ = +0.00** on Qwen and **-0.24 (p = 0.36)** on Devstral. The collapse holds across 14 metric variants: KLD aggregations, forms of perplexity, top-1 agreement, calibration corpora and context lengths. On LiveCodeBench, per-prompt KLD barely separates failures from successes (ratio 1.08-1.22). The authors sum up: KLD measures *how much* a quantization deviates from the reference, not *in which direction* it deviates ([arXiv 2606.19558](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19558)).

For pancake, this corrects a rule in the plan, it does not just add a paper. The gate ladder for lossy items is "token forcing → BFCL v4 multi-turn + LiveCodeBench → HELMET → Terminal-Bench". Items 8, 13 and 23 are described as "gated by token forcing", and Q2_0 was characterized by **80.6% same top token as Q8_0** ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). Token forcing is a top-1 agreement metric, so it is exactly the kind of metric the paper shows to be blind in the quiet zone. The correct rule becomes: token forcing rejects variants that deviate a lot (there the correlation is real), but the decision between close candidates is made by a fixed set of coding and agent tasks. This matters for Q2_0 vs IQ2_XS, for the DASLab Coder variant (item 25), for the sparsity thresholds in item 13 and for the deferral in item 8. It also follows that SpAx or TEAL thresholds should not be calibrated on WikiText perplexity, as their authors do.

Two sources complete the metric. **SWE-Effi** (Huawei Canada lead, Ahmed E. Hassan of Queen's co) evaluates agents by the area under the resolve-rate curve, under budgets of tokens, cost and inference time. The study finds a "token snowball": input grows roughly linearly with each call, up to **~8.1M input tokens in 181 calls** for SWE-Agent with GPT-4o-mini. Unresolved runs cost **~8.8M tokens / 658 s, against ~1.8M / 167 s** for resolved ones ([alphaXiv 2509.09853](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.09853.md)). The area under the time curve is the right metric for changes that move both speed and success, such as thinking budgets, the loop guard or a lossy variant. **MMLU-Pro** (Waterloo TIGER-Lab lead, [arXiv 2406.01574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01574)) is a cheap reasoning check with 10 answer options. It is not a coding task, so it can only be an extra step, not a replacement for LiveCodeBench.

## Failed agents cost four times as much: the loop guard can see behavior

The UBC study **"Beyond Accuracy"** (Mesbah lead, Gallaba and Hassan of Queen's co) ran 4 agents on 372 multi-hunk Java bugs, 1,488 trajectories in total. **Qwen Code fixed only 25.81%**, against 93.28% for Claude Code. Failed runs used **39-343% more tokens and 43-427% more time**. The authors describe three failure patterns: repeated edits without validation (Gemini-cli), exploration without action (Claude Code) and navigation cost (Qwen Code) ([arXiv 2511.11012](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11012v1)). On a machine that decodes at ~126 t/s, a runaway session means tens of minutes.

The loop guard ported from Maya catches blocks of text repeated one after another, identical tool results received 3 times, and conversations that end with nothing new ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md), Project Maya section). It does not catch the two behavioral patterns from the study, because each call differs from the previous one. The extension is also on the server, with no engine changes. On the conversation's tool history it would count two things: **N consecutive edits with no build or test command** and **M consecutive reads or searches with no edit**. When a counter exceeds its threshold, the server would inject a note, through the same mechanism it already uses after a repeated result. The thresholds do not come from the paper. I would calibrate them from our own opencode logs, running first in logging-only mode. The escalation "fresh-context review when the loop guard fires" from the MIT/Harvard report (action 9) is the natural step after the second note.

The second signal comes from thinking. **DeepSeek-R1 Thoughtology** (Siva Reddy, Mila/McGill lead) finds an optimal reasoning length for each problem, beyond which performance drops. Correct thoughts are on average much shorter than wrong ones. Rumination, that is, returning to approaches already tried, is measured through the **5-gram repetition rate** and normalized lexical entropy. It grows with difficulty ([arXiv 2504.07128](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07128v3)). A 5-gram rate over a sliding thinking window costs O(1) per token, with a hash and a circular counter. Unlike the loop guard, it catches returns that are not consecutive. It should first be used only as a logged signal and as a feature for the learned probe in item 21. Only then could it trigger soft close (`reasoning_budget_grace`) earlier, when the level's budget is already mostly used and rumination is high. The paper only says rumination seems negatively associated with accuracy and gives no threshold. The budget figures are only in plots, so the threshold must be measured on our traces.

**ECLoop** (Tse-Hsun Chen, Concordia lead) tackles premature commitment. From the issue text and the repo structure it builds the evidence the agent must see before each type of edit or hand-off, and it defers actions until the agent has seen it. The gain is **+4.8-11.8 pp Pass@1 on all 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks**, with up to **12.1% fewer tokens** ([arXiv 2607.28815](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28815)). A minimal version for opencode would be a plugin or a tool wrapper with two rules: the file must be read before editing, and a test must be run before the agent declares the task done. It goes together with the semantic constraint on `oldString` from item 26. Before building, check what opencode already enforces at edit time. **STAIR** (same group) turns past fixes into trees of abstract plans and raises mini-SWE-agent v2 from **75.8% to 81.0%** with no code changes. Raw, non-abstracted trajectories transfer much worse ([arXiv 2607.29658](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29658)). A single user who works repeatedly on the same repos is the ideal case. The hints must, however, go after the common system and tool prefix, not inside it, so they do not break the shared prefix from item 11.

The other agent papers fit our situation less well. **Aegis** (Pekhimenko, Toronto lead) raises the success rate by **6.7-12.5%** through changes to the environment only: better observability, common computations offloaded from the agent, and speculative actions ([arXiv 2508.19504](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19504)). The observability part (richer error messages from tools) ties in with tolerant tool-call recovery. **SpecCache** (Wisconsin lead, Toronto co) hides web environment latency, which reaches 53.7% of the total ([arXiv 2510.16276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16276)). For us, local tools take milliseconds and decoding dominates, so the gain is small. The useful tool window is already tracked by item 17, for experts. **Markovian Thinker / Delethink** (Mila lead, ICLR 2026) splits thinking into fixed-size chunks. At each boundary, the context resets to the prompt plus a short carryover. Models from 1.5B to 120B often produce such traces zero-shot ([arXiv 2510.06557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06557)). **Robust Reasoning Benchmark** (Toronto lead) observes attention dilution across successive problems in the same context ([arXiv 2604.08571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08571)). Delethink's cost argument (quadratic attention) weakens here, where 3 of 4 layers are GDN with constant state. A mid-turn reset would be a restore of a boundary checkpoint, which the conversation cache already supports. It is worth testing only if the logs show turns with more than ~16K thinking tokens, which the per-level budgets make rare.

## The engine gets a new mask and a reasoned "no"

**SpAx** (Vijaykumar, Toronto lead, Oct. 2026) is the only Canadian paper built on llama.cpp at batch 1 with weights offloaded from VRAM. Each weight column is read either at full precision (for large activations), as a 2-bit approximation (for the middle band), or not at all (for activations near zero). Selection is a per-token top-k, with per-layer budgets calibrated offline ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02598v1)). The gain drops with the base precision: **3.86x on BF16, but 2.06x at 4 bits** with offload to RAM and **1.54x at 4 bits** with offload to NVMe ([arXiv 2610.02598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02598)). The tested models are dense only; there are no MoE results and no published code. Pancake's Q2_0 experts are already at ~2 bits and are computed on the CPU, not transferred. The approximate 2-bit level therefore saves nothing here. Two useful parts remain. The first is a fourth mask in the offline pass from item 13, next to the Berkeley gate mask, TEAL and CATS (plus CHESS from the Asian report, M2): a top-k on the intermediate activation (the SwiGLU output) that goes into `down_proj`. It uses the same AVX2 gather kernel with W_down repacked along n_ff. The second is partial reads only for experts in the NVMe tier, where the paper's flash results apply. The quality risk is real: at perplexity parity with TEAL, both methods lose ~6 GSM8K points against the dense model ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02598v1)). This is one more reason for the item 13 gate to be task-based, per the section above.

**Cactus** (Hao and Mou, Alberta/Amii lead, ICLR 2026) reformulates speculative acceptance as optimization with a KL budget δ against the verifier. The result is a closed-form increase in the probability of the proposed token. On Qwen3-14B with a 0.6B drafter, accepted length rises **from 4.27 to 5.44**, and on Spec-Bench the speedup is **1.88x against 1.81x** with standard SpS ([alphaXiv 2604.04987](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.04987.md)). In pancake, the probabilistic acceptance rule applies only to MTP-drawn windows in sampled mode (`mtp_q_applies` in `include/strata/spec/window_rule.hpp`). Lookup windows are point masses. The user runs at **T=0.1** by explicit choice, and the recommendation is not to change that. Under greedy, Cactus changes nothing (Γ(0) = 0). At T=0.1, the verifier distribution is so sharp that the proposed token has either probability near 1 (accepted anyway) or near 0. In the second case, making it acceptable would need a large KL budget, which is exactly the quality loss δ is meant to limit. The real gain would therefore be almost zero. There is one more argument: longer accepted chains touch more distinct experts per verification, the same concern as with AcceptMoE in item 23. If Cactus is ever built, it will be opt-in, lossy and with a cap on extra misses per verification. Until then it stays parked.

Contextual drafter selection as a bandit (**Huang, ..., Chandar, Mila/Polytechnique lead**, EMNLP 2024, [arXiv 2408.08470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08470)) does not need a new item. DISCO already picks the length with a feature classifier. The useful lesson is to widen its action space to the draft source (MTP, prompt-lookup or `SuffixDrafter`), with features such as code vs text, echo from a tool output, or recent acceptance. This is worth doing only if the `DraftPolicy` logs show frequent wrong choices. Su and Pekhimenko's adaptive length ([arXiv 2310.18813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18813)) reduces at batch 1 to what `DraftPolicy` already does. EAGLE-2's dynamic confidence loop (Waterloo co) is covered by `--spec-min-p`.

On the expert cache, Canada has nothing of its own. FineMoE (Waterloo co) is already in the US and MIT/Harvard reports. The closest learned-cache result, FlashMoE (affiliation probably KAIST, not confirmed), gives **only +7% over LRU on Qwen3-30B-A3B** ([arXiv 2601.17063](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17063v1)). The notes propose measuring the distance to Belady. It is already measured in pancake (89.5% LRU vs 94.7% Belady), and item 14 handles it with the PCIe 3.0 promotion cost. So no new action appears here.

The order follows a precise logic. M1 is a rule, not a project, and it must be fixed before any remaining lossy decision: items 8, 13 and 23, the Coder variant and any new preset. A1 and A2 extend a mechanism that is already on. They start in logging mode, so they cannot break anything, and M3 gives them their thresholds. A3 is the only agent action with a published gain on the order of ten points on a repair benchmark, but it needs an opencode plugin and a check of what the editor already does. P1 and P2 are not new items, but extra columns and actions in work already planned. Explicitly skipped: SLiM, PATCH, OPTIMA, Hexcute, Tilus, InnerQ, FoX/ACP, Proteus, Seesaw, CRAFT, ScatterMoE, stick-breaking attention, minGRU, RSA, Scorpio, RL^V, DARS, AceCoder, VerlTool, implicit CoT, KV-streams, CodePromptZip, EAGLE-3 and FlashMoE.

A few gaps remain. I found no Canadian paper on expert offload, caching or prediction at batch 1, on speculation for GDN hybrid models, or on ROCm kernels. The affiliation search, however, is unreliable outside Montreal and Toronto. For SpAx there is no code, no MoE results and no results below 4 bits. For Delethink I did not see the size of the carryover or the per-model zero-shot rates. The Thoughtology budget figures are only in plots. I did not check whether opencode already enforces reading before editing. Next comes the last round: the Pacific, Africa and Latin America.

## Conclusion

The series started out looking for engine speed, and Canada brings the clearest argument that the next daily gain is no longer in tokens per second. Pancake already beats Avalon and stock Strata. On the agent side, however, the UBC and Queen's studies show that a failed session costs about as much as four successful ones. On a local machine, that is a bigger loss than any percent left in the kernels. The Maya loop guard was the first step. The behavioral counters and the rumination signal take it from catching repeated text to catching an agent that goes in circles. Both are on the server, cheap and reversible.

The second lesson is less comfortable, because it touches a practice already in use. The series has accumulated promising lossy options: the deferral in item 8, the sparsity in item 13, the verification priority in item 23 and the turbo preset. All were validated through token forcing, a metric the Moshovos paper shows to be blind exactly in the zone where these options sit. If the rule in M1 does not change, the choice between close variants stays practically random.
