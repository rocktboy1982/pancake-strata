# MIT corrects the Stanford plan for pancake

MIT brings more than Stanford for our engine, but it still brings no decode speedup that is ready to port. Three contributions matter. **TEAL** (MIT-lead) **replaces CATS** as the basis for the sparsity experiment on the experts computed on the CPU. **The Gated DeltaNet / flash-linear-attention line** (Songlin Yang, Yoon Kim) describes exactly the layer that pancake already runs and points to the only kernel tuning left (chunk 64 with block inversion, versus the chunk of 16 in the gfx11 WMMA path). **Hidden-state probes that stop thinking** (Thought Calibration, OS-Pruner, Learning How Hard to Think) turn the fixed `reasoning_budget_by_effort` cap into a learned decision. CATS breaks down at 40% sparsity on Llama-3-8B (PPL ~7.6e4), while TEAL holds at 6.21 versus 5.87 dense. That is why the CPU experiment must be done TEAL-style: first measure the activation distribution on the Qwen experts, calibrate on code, and re-pack W_down. The estimated gain is 10-15% on decode, if sparsity exists on fine-grained MoE experts, which nobody has tested. On the agent side, **Recursive Language Models** (MIT CSAIL) is the strongest argument so far for keeping large tool outputs out of the prompt instead of compacting them: +26% median versus compaction. The rest of the MIT portfolio (SmoothQuant, AWQ, QServe, SVDQuant, StreamingLLM, DuoAttention, LServe, FlashMoBA, PASTA, SEAL) is either already covered by ggml and pancake, built for large batches on NVIDIA, or dependent on retraining the 125B model. Harvard contributes mostly theory: Scaling Laws for Precision explains why IQ2_XS on an over-trained model like Qwen is fragile, and Mixture of Parrots warns that pruning experts costs knowledge, not reasoning. All numbers are the authors', obtained on other hardware and other models. Nothing below has been measured on our machine yet.

## How I read the papers: the CPU decides the decode window

**Lead** means the first or last author is at MIT or Harvard. **Co** means MIT/Harvard appear only among the co-authors. **Inferred** means the affiliation comes from known lab membership (Han Lab, CSAIL, Kempner) and was not read in the PDF. The verdicts start from pancake's measured profile: in prefill, GDN takes ~22%, attention 16% and MMQ experts 30%; in decode, the GPU works 15.9 ms out of a 24.8 ms window, and the rest is dominated by the experts computed on the CPU. The pancake plan confirms the same pattern from the 3060 era: a GDN megakernel gave **0.0 ms gain on a single GPU, because the CPU experts dictate the window** ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). The consequence is simple. In decode, only what shortens CPU expert time or the number of generated tokens matters. In prefill, GDN, attention and experts matter, in that order of ease.

I do not re-recommend what pancake already has: chunked GDN prefill on gfx11 matrix cores, MTP + prompt-lookup, DraftPolicy (adaptive draft length and the choice of MTP vs suffix by measured cost and acceptance), the parked conversation cache with checkpoints at message boundaries, INT8 KV, the global thinking budget and `reasoning_budget_by_effort` (commit 5dd620fb9), repeat stop, recovery of reasoning loops and tool calls, `env_to_user` and the loop guard being ported. I also do not repeat tests already done without gain: HIP graphs, unroll, the COMPUTE profile, draft p-min, checkpoint tuning, Vulkan, KV turbo4/TurboQuant, L3 prefetch (`STRATA_ARENA_WARM`) and ClassLRU scoring.

## TEAL overturns the CATS recommendation for the CPU experts

The Stanford report called CATS "the best engine idea". The MIT notes show that the idea is good but the variant is wrong. **TEAL** (James Liu, Han Guo, Yoon Kim from MIT, with Together AI and Tianle Cai from Princeton; ICLR 2025) applies a magnitude threshold on the **input** of all seven matrices (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down), not only on the MLP. Thresholds are calibrated offline, and per-layer sparsity is chosen by a greedy block-wise optimization, under one A100 hour for Llama-3-8B ([arXiv 2408.14690](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)). The quality results are what matter: on WikiText, Llama-3-8B goes from **5.87 (dense) to 5.94 / 6.21 / 6.67 at 25 / 40 / 50% sparsity**, and Llama-3-70B from 2.93 to 3.52 at 40%. **CATS at 40% gives PPL ~7.6e4 on Llama-3-8B, that is, it degenerates** ([arXiv 2408.14690](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3), compared with [CATS](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08763v4)). On speed, TEAL's sparse GEMV kernel gives 1.42x / 1.63x at 40 / 50% on Llama-3-8B on an A6000, but the gain shrinks on the A100, which has more bandwidth, and at 0% sparsity the kernel is slower than the dense one (0.91x). One detail matters for us: TEAL was also tested on top of **2- and 3-bit QuIP#**, and the perplexity breaking point stays at a similar sparsity, so the errors compound roughly independently. The limits are just as clear: **no experiment on MoE, none on CPU**, and attention-sink tokens must be protected.

For pancake, this changes the experiment design in four points. First: sparsity is applied first on the input of **down_proj** (the expert's intermediate activation), because skipping input channels means skipping columns of W_down. This saves bytes only if W_down is re-packed at load time in n_ff-major order. It is the same re-pack proposed for CATS, so the layout code is not lost. Second: the threshold is calibrated on code and on real opencode traces, not on C4. Third: per-layer sparsity is chosen with TEAL's greedy method, not with a global threshold. Fourth: the gate/up input (the hidden state x) is shared by all experts of a layer, but its zeros fall in super-blocks of 256 elements, so a gain there requires skipping whole blocks or reordering channels. That stays a second stage.

The estimate from the notes, computed on our numbers and unsourced as such: with ~40% sparsity on the down input only, a CPU expert reads roughly 13% fewer bytes. With block-level skipping on gate/up as well, it can reach 25-40% of CPU expert time. The non-GPU part of the window is at most ~8.9 ms (24.8 − 15.9), and the window cannot drop below the GPU's ~16 ms without other changes. The realistic ceiling is therefore **around 10-15% on decode**, and the down-only variant probably gives half of that. The main risk is the one also identified in the Stanford report: fine-grained experts (small n_ff, only k of N active) may be less sparse than dense 7-70B MLPs. On the GPU, where the IQ2_XS experts are already small and the k-quant blocks pack 256 consecutive channels, TEAL saves almost no bytes. That is why the first step is cheap and decisive. Pancake already has token-forcing verification and the mismatch rate against stock noise (1.17-1.30%), used for cache-aware routing ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). Activations below the threshold can be zeroed in FP32, with no new kernel, and mismatch and KL measured on replayed real opencode traces, at 25 / 40 / 50%. The AVX2 kernel is written only if 40% passes the bar.

### MIT quantization does not beat IQ2_XS, but Harvard explains why IQ2_XS hurts

Han Lab built SmoothQuant, AWQ, QServe and SVDQuant for 4-8 bits and for throughput on NVIDIA. At batch 1 and 2-3 bpw, decode is limited by weight read bandwidth, and ggml already does the useful part: activation-aware importance through imatrix and q8 activations in the CPU dot products ([arXiv 2306.00978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978), [arXiv 2211.10438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438)). The KV4 part of QServe is the only one that looks portable. But the pancake plan measured that **extra VRAM does not move decode**: +5% cache slots changed nothing, and "KV body" was dropped with the note "VRAM does not buy decode here" ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). So QServe does not enter the plan.

Scaling Laws for Precision (Harvard-lead) instead gives a direct argument about the file we run every day. **Degradation after post-training quantization grows with the number of pre-training tokens** and shrinks with model size. The law was fitted on more than 465 runs, up to 1.7B parameters ([arXiv 2411.04330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330)). Independent work confirms the direction: "Low-Bit Quantization Favors Undertrained LLMs" finds the same fragility in well-trained models ([arXiv 2411.17691](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.17691)), and ParetoQ reports a learning transition between 2 and 3 bits ([arXiv 2502.02631](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2502.02631)). Qwen is trained on trillions of tokens, and pancake runs IQ2_XS. The extrapolation, unsourced for a 125B MoE, is that IQ3 would be much safer than IQ2 precisely on the hot experts, which are read most often. The pancake plan notes that mixed quantization by expert sensitivity needs the BF16 model (~250 GB), impossible on this disk ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). There is an untested workaround, though: experts can be mixed per tensor from two already quantized GGUFs (IQ3 for the hot experts from the learned profile, IQ2_XS for the cold ones), with the LQ-LoRA ILP as the allocator under a byte budget. The path depends on an IQ3 file existing, and the Avalon model is now deleted. I put it at low priority, with a code quality test before any speed gain.

Mixture of Parrots (Harvard-lead) shows that adding experts keeps improving memorization, while the reasoning gain saturates ([arXiv 2410.19034](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.19034v2)). For us it is a warning: if we ever prune rare experts REAP-style to fit more in RAM, we will first lose the memory of APIs and libraries, which is exactly what a coding agent uses. The evaluation must therefore include API recall, not only reasoning benchmarks. OEA (Harvard-lead) cuts decode latency by 39% on a 30B MoE at batch 16, by re-routing tokens to experts already loaded by other tokens in the batch ([alphaXiv 2511.02237](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.02237)). Pancake already tested the "toward resident experts" variant (`STRATA_ROUTE_PRIOR`): λ=2.0 was rejected with 9.90% mismatch, and λ=0.5 gave +25% on decode with 2.9% mismatch, as a `turbo` option ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). OEA adds only one untested variant: in the verify window, the bonus goes to the experts already chosen by sibling tokens in the same window, which shrinks the union of missed experts. It is evaluated with the same mismatch harness. The gain is small and uncertain.

## Gated DeltaNet is our layer; only kernel tuning is left

The Yang/Kim line at MIT defines the algorithm that pancake already runs. **GLA** introduced the hardware-efficient chunkwise form and FLASHLINEARATTENTION ([arXiv 2312.06635](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06635)). **Parallelizing Linear Transformers with the Delta Rule** turned the DeltaNet recurrence into a triangular solve plus dense matmuls per chunk, through the WY representation, without materializing per-token states ([arXiv 2406.06484](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.06484v4)). **Gated DeltaNet** (first author Yang, MIT, with NVIDIA; ICLR 2025) combined the scalar gating from Mamba2 with the delta rule ([arXiv 2412.06464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06464)). The comments in `src/prefill/gdn_chunk.cu` describe exactly this scheme, so pancake has nothing new to port algorithmically.

The remaining difference is the chunk size. The gfx11 WMMA path (`STRATA_GDN_CHUNKED=1`) works **in chunks of 16 tokens** (the comment at `kernels.cu:1803`), and the CUDA path uses `GCH = 32`. The WY/UT form allows C = 64. The 64x64 strictly lower triangular matrix A = β·exp(Δg)·KKᵀ is inverted by blocks: the 16x16 diagonal blocks are handled directly, and the off-diagonal ones through WMMA matmuls. This cuts the serial steps of the inter-chunk scan by 4x. The prep kernels (cumsum, KKᵀ, the solve, the W/U recompute) can be fused into a single one per (chunk, head), because they are parallel across chunks. The "late-to-early decay" form in `gdn_chunk.cu` keeps factors ≤ 1, so FP16 stays safe. The cost is more LDS. For reference, AMD validated FLA on MI300X (182,729 versus 183,213 tok/s/GPU with Primus) ([ROCm Primus GDN](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/projects/primus/en/latest/04-technical-guides/hybrid-models/gdn-guide.html)), but had problems compiling some Triton variants for KDA ([ROCm Primus KDA](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/projects/primus/en/latest/04-technical-guides/hybrid-models/kda-guide.html)). There is also a HIP + rocWMMA repository for RDNA4, useful as a comparison baseline for the gfx12 path ([HF gdn-hip](https://huggingface.co/pat883/gdn-hip)). Before the redesign, `chunk_gated_delta_rule` from fla-org must be read, to confirm chunk 64 and the solve_tril / recompute_w_u stages. **At 22% of prefill, a GDN kernel 1.3-2x faster brings 5-11% on total prefill.** In decode it brings nothing, because of the CPU-dominated window.

The rest of the line needs retraining and does not apply to the shipped Qwen weights: PaTH Attention ([arXiv 2505.16381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16381v1)), Log-Linear Attention ([arXiv 2506.04761](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04761v2)), distillation into hybrids by KL selection ([arXiv 2512.20569](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.20569v1)), plus the co-authored Kimi Linear/KDA ([arXiv 2510.26692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26692)), Jet-Nemotron ([arXiv 2508.15884](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15884v3)) and Stick-breaking ([arXiv 2410.17980](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17980)).

### Han Lab attention is approximate; only XAttention deserves an opt-in mode

All Han Lab methods for long context evict, sparsify or skip KV, so none is exact. For an agent that must find exact strings anywhere in a 131k prompt, StreamingLLM ([arXiv 2309.17453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453)) and the "streaming" heads of DuoAttention ([arXiv 2410.10819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10819)) lose precisely the middle of the context, where the files and tool outputs sit. DuoAttention also needs a gradient optimization on the model. Harvard gives the theoretical reason why full-attention layers must not be touched aggressively: **Repeat After Me** proves that fixed-state models fail at copying and retrieval from long context ([arXiv 2402.01032](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01032)). FlashMoBA needs a model trained with MoBA ([arXiv 2511.11571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11571v1)).

**XAttention** is the only realistic candidate for prefill. It scores (query block, key block) pairs by summing antidiagonal elements with a fixed stride, then runs block-sparse attention only on the chosen blocks. The method needs no training and reports up to **13.5x at 256K versus FlashAttention** ([arXiv 2503.16428](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16428v1)). Porting it into the existing flash-attention tile loop needs only scoring and a tile-skip mask, with no new math. Diagonal blocks and sink blocks are always kept. At usual lengths, attention takes 16% of prefill. It grows with context, so at 131k+ it can become the main component. With a conservative threshold τ, the notes estimate 20-40% of prefill on the longest prompts, unmeasured. The mode must be opt-in above ~64k and guarded by a "needle in a haystack" regression test on real code. **Quest** does query-guided KV page selection in decode (2.23x on self-attention) ([arXiv 2406.10774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10774)), but our decode is not attention-bound, so the end-to-end gain would be a few percent. It stays at "after profiling". From LServe, only the idea of reusing the selected page set over several steps can be taken ([arXiv 2502.14866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14866)). The parked conversation cache already removes most of the repeated prefill, so XAttention only helps new, long or cold segments.

## Hidden-state probes turn the thinking cap into a decision

This is where MIT adds the most over Stanford. The Stanford report justified a hard thinking cap (s1), and pancake already has it, including per effort level. MIT shows how to stop thinking **when it no longer adds anything**, not just at a fixed token count. **Thought Calibration** (Wu, Zhou, Bates, Jaakkola; MIT-lead inferred) treats thinking as a growing reasoning tree and stops when the tree reaches a plateau. Linear probes on the hidden representations make the decision. On 3 reasoning models and 4 datasets, thinking tokens drop by **up to 60% in distribution and up to 20% out of distribution**, with no performance loss ([arXiv 2505.18404](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18404)). **OS-Pruner** (MIT/Columbia, individual attribution unconfirmed; NeurIPS 2026 poster) frames the same decision as an optimal stopping problem at each paragraph boundary, with utility accuracy − λ·tokens. On R1-Distill-Qwen-7B / GSM8K it gets 88.0% with 496 tokens, versus 88.7% with 1,219: 59.3% fewer tokens for 0.7 points. On AIME, however, accuracy drops from 44.4% to 36.9%, and the method retrains the last two layers, not just a linear head ([alphaXiv 2607.11089](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.11089)). **Learning How Hard to Think** (Damani, ..., Andreas; MIT-lead; ICLR 2025) predicts per input the marginal benefit of extra compute. It reports up to 50% less compute at equal quality on math and code ([ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/ff414825df833edb8b1839e3d5d495e9-Abstract-Conference.html)).

One detail from the pancake plan matters. Dynasor-style early exit was postponed because it "needs a forked probe of the single decode stream" ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). **Thought Calibration removes the objection**: the probe reads the hidden state of the current stream, and each check costs one dot product, so no fork is needed. The recipe has four steps. One: save hidden states at fixed intervals (for example every 64 thinking tokens or at `\n\n`) over a few hundred replayed opencode turns. Two: label each point by the question "if we forced `</think>` here, would the tool call or final answer have been the same?", using the replay pancake already has. Three: train a linear probe offline. Four: at run time, the server injects `</think>` when the probe is confident, and the `reasoning_budget_by_effort` cap stays as the safety net. At ~126 t/s, a 1,500-token thought cut by a third saves ~4 s per turn. The OOD figure of ≤20% is the prudent bound, because opencode traces are outside the paper's math and QA distribution. The AIME warning says the probe should be used at `low`/`medium`, not on hard debugging. Learning How Hard to Think gives the complementary half: a probe on the last hidden state of the prompt, which picks the effort level before generation. This way the hand-written rule from the Stanford report ("escalate on a tool error or a failed test") becomes a policy learned from our own sessions, replayed at the three levels. **None of the papers evaluated agentic coding turns**, and nobody has tested the effect of early stopping on MTP acceptance.

On speculative decoding, MIT confirms that pancake is already there. **Hydra** (MIT-lead) shows that a draft head that sees the previously speculated tokens beats independent Medusa heads: 1.31x over Medusa, 2.70x over autoregressive decoding ([arXiv 2402.05109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05109)). Qwen's MTP head already works this way, so only a small top-2 tree is left. That is expensive for us, because each branch activates different experts, and the pancake plan measures the draft at 2-5% of a window ([MOD_PLAN](file:///home/dan/clone-of-d/src/Strata/MOD_PLAN.md)). **TLT** (Han Lab) proposes choosing the speculation strategy online by measured acceptance ([arXiv 2511.16665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16665)), and that is exactly what DraftPolicy does. PASTA ([arXiv 2502.11517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11517)) and Co-LLM ([arXiv 2403.03870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03870)) need fine-tuning on the target model. **No MIT or Harvard paper deals with recurrent state rollback during speculation on hybrid models.** ReplaySSM (NVIDIA/Princeton) reports 1.85-2.10x on 120B-class models ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/replayssm-rethinking-state-storage-for-faster-hybrid-model-decoding/)), but it belongs to the Princeton/companies report.

## Recursive Language Models take large context out of the prompt

On the agent side, the most relevant MIT paper is **Recursive Language Models** (Zhang, Kraska, Khattab, MIT CSAIL). The long prompt sits as a variable in a persistent REPL, and the model writes code to inspect and split it, then calls itself recursively on the pieces. With GPT-5 as the root model, the median gain is **+26% versus compaction and +13% versus Claude Code**, at comparable cost. The authors also post-trained an **RLM-Qwen3-8B, with +28.3% over base Qwen3-8B** ([arXiv 2512.24601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24601)). The `alexzhang13/rlm` library comes from MIT's OASYS lab ([GitHub](https://github.com/alexzhang13/rlm)). On our hardware, the benefit doubles. The prompt stays short, so prefill (expensive with experts on the CPU) drops. Sub-calls have a stable system prefix, so the parked conversation cache stays warm. The Stanford report recommended delta compaction (ACE). RLM does not contradict it, but splits it in two: **the narrative is compacted, and bulky data (logs, large diffs, whole files) is externalized** to disk or into a REPL variable and queried on demand. In opencode this means a "RLM" tool or sub-agent for tasks that exceed 131k. I have not checked whether the library works directly with an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, like pancake. There is no independent replication yet.

**MEM1** (MIT/NUS/SMART/Yonsei, lead unconfirmed; ICLR 2026) trains through RL a compact internal state, rewritten at every turn, instead of a history that only grows. MEM1-7B gets 3.5x the performance and 3.7x less memory than Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct on a task with 16 objectives ([arXiv 2506.15841](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15841v2)). The training is not practical for the main model, but the pattern can be used without it: a fixed-size state block (goal, facts, TODOs, last error) placed in the last user message, where `env_to_user` already puts the variable state, so it does not invalidate the prefix.

On reliability, **Is Self-Repair a Silver Bullet?** (Olausson, ..., Solar-Lezama; MIT-lead, with Microsoft; ICLR 2024) shows that self-repair gives modest gains once you count its cost, and that it is limited by feedback quality. Feedback from a stronger model or from humans, however, brings large gains, even for GPT-4 ([arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896)). This supports two rules. First: effort is not escalated on "re-read your code" loops without a new external signal (failed test, compile error, tool error). Second: when the loop guard fires, a review pass with fresh context helps more than another attempt in the same context. **CodeSteer and R1-Code-Interpreter** (Yongchao Chen et al., MIT/Harvard) show that the choice between running code and reasoning in text can be learned and steered from outside. R1-CI-14B rises from 44.1% to 72.4% on 37 test tasks, above GPT-4o with Code Interpreter (70.9%) ([arXiv 2505.21668](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21668v3)). The cheap version for us is one prompt line: "when a claim can be checked by running code or a test, run it".

Test-time adaptation (SEAL, [arXiv 2506.10943](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2); TTT for ARC, [arXiv 2411.07279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279v1)) needs gradients through the adapted model, so it cannot be done on 125B. **RL's Razor** (MIT-lead; ICLR 2026) is the useful guide if local helpers come back: at the same accuracy on the new task, RL forgets less than SFT, and KL to the base model predicts forgetting ([arXiv 2509.04259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04259)). Concretely, a helper is trained on its own scored samples, not by SFT on the outputs of the 125B model. Echo Chamber (Harvard-lead) adds that RL narrows the helper toward the dominant style from pre-training ([arXiv 2504.07912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07912)). DisCIPL (MIT-lead), with a large planner and small followers, fits our hardware only for parallelizable, constrained sub-tasks ([arXiv 2504.07081](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07081)). All three are low priority while `small_model` is a cloud model.

### Measurement: compute-quality curves, not isolated numbers

Neither MIT nor Harvard has a benchmark for multi-turn coding agents comparable to Terminal-Bench, which stays the baseline from the Stanford report. Three MIT tools complete the harness. The protocol from Learning How Hard to Think compares compute at equal quality, and every change in effort or thinking budget must be reported as a success rate / thinking tokens curve, not as a single number ([ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/ff414825df833edb8b1839e3d5d495e9-Abstract-Conference.html)). The long-context tasks from RLM serve to test the RLM tool and the compaction policy ([arXiv 2512.24601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24601)). The MEM1 recipe of composing single-objective tasks into 16-objective tasks ([arXiv 2506.15841](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15841v2)) can be applied to our own repositories, for example "8 questions about different files in the same session". For any lossy change (TEAL, routing, stop probe), pancake already has token-forcing verification with mismatch against stock noise. It must stay the entry gate, before Terminal-Bench.

The order has a precise logic. #1 is a one-day measurement that decides the largest possible decode gain in the whole MIT portfolio. #3 targets tokens, the only other lever that shortens CPU-dominated windows. #4 and #5 work on prefill, where the GPU really matters. Explicitly skipped, for the reasons in the sections above: SmoothQuant, AWQ, QServe (including KV4), SVDQuant, StreamingLLM, DuoAttention, FlashMoBA, PaTH, Log-Linear, Jet-Nemotron, PASTA, Co-LLM, the separate Hydra tree, TLT (already DraftPolicy), Reasoning with Sampling, SEAL and TTT on the main model, plus a GDN megakernel for decode (0.0 ms already measured).

### What is left for the next reports

The notes confirm gaps that MIT and Harvard do not cover and that must be searched elsewhere. **Expert offload, caching, prefetch and router prediction** (MoE-Infinity from Edinburgh, fMoE from Stevens/Rice, ExpertFlow, BuddyMoE from SJTU, MELINOE from CMU) go to the CMU and Europe/Asia reports ([fMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05370v1), [ExpertFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.17954v2)). **Recurrent state rollback during speculation** (ReplaySSM, STree, SpecLA, TreeWY) goes to Princeton and to the companies ([Tri Dao blog](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/replayssm/), [arXiv 2505.14969](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.14969)). **2-3 bit quantization** (QuIP#/QTIP) goes to Cornell. Lookahead/Jacobi decoding goes to UCSD. **Expert pruning** (REAP, which shows that below 4 bits the quantization + pruning combination beats more aggressive quantization) ([arXiv 2510.13999](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999v2)) goes to the company reports. For Harvard, the searches found no LLM serving system in the window. The Harvard systems groups and the MIT-IBM lists for 2026 were not covered systematically, so lesser-known papers may be missing.

## Conclusion

Stanford moved the question from tokens per second to seconds per turn. MIT brings it partly back into the engine, but only in two narrow places: the activations that can be skipped on the CPU experts and the serial steps of the GDN scan in prefill. Both come from Yoon Kim's group, not from Han Lab, which produced most of MIT's efficiency papers, but for another regime (large batch, NVIDIA, 4-8 bits). For a single-user machine with experts on the CPU, the useful question about a paper is whether it reduces the bytes read by the CPU or the tokens generated. TEAL and the stop probes are the only ones that answer yes.

The correction to the Stanford report is also methodological. CATS looked sufficient on the basis of a single paper, and the direct comparison in TEAL shows that the gate threshold collapses exactly at the sparsity we need. The same can happen to TEAL on fine MoE experts: no paper has tested it there, and Scaling Laws for Precision suggests that an over-trained model, already at IQ2_XS, has less margin for compounded errors. Pancake already has the token-forcing mismatch harness, which turns any lossy idea into a decision measurable in one day. That is in fact the most valuable asset for the rest of the series.
