# MIT and Harvard papers on quantization, sparsity, MoE efficiency and offloading (Nov 2022 - Nov 2026), judged for pancake/Avalon

Context: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (~125B MoE hybrid) as IQ2_XS / IQ3_KS GGUF. RX 7900 XT 20 GB (ROCm 10), Ryzen 7 5700G AVX2 at ~28-42 GB/s RAM, 57 GB RAM. Experts are tiered VRAM (~44%) / pinned RAM / NVMe, and the CPU computes the missed experts, which is RAM-bandwidth bound. KV is q8_0, batch 1.
Lead/co flag: "MIT-lead" / "Harvard-lead" means the first author and/or the senior author is at that institution. Excluded: attention kernels, serving and decoding, agent papers. Items already in the Stanford report: CATS is Stanford-led and not repeated, except where TEAL contradicts it. Scaling Laws for Precision is Harvard-led, so it is covered here in more depth.

## Q1. MIT Han Lab quantization (AWQ, SmoothQuant, QServe, SVDQuant, KV quant), plus other MIT quantization work: does any of it beat our 2-3-bit GGUF experts or speed up CPU expert compute?

### Takeaway
None of the Han Lab methods beats IQ2_XS/IQ3_KS GGUF at our bit widths or speeds up batch-1 CPU expert decode. They target 4-8-bit weights and/or INT8/INT4 activations on NVIDIA for throughput serving. ggml already does the useful parts (activation-aware importance via imatrix, q8 activations in CPU dot products). The only items worth a look are cheap side options: 4-bit KV (QServe's SmoothAttention) to free VRAM for the expert cache, LUT-kernel ideas from FLUTE for the HIP IQ kernels, and ILP-style per-tensor bit allocation (LQ-LoRA) as an offline tool.

### Cited Findings
- **SmoothQuant** (MIT-lead; Guangxuan Xiao, Ji Lin, ..., Song Han; with NVIDIA). arXiv 2211.10438, Nov 2022, ICML 2023. Moves activation outliers into the weights with a per-channel smoothing scale, which enables W8A8 INT8. Code: github.com/mit-han-lab/smoothquant. — [arXiv 2211.10438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438)
  - Verdict: not portable as a gain. Our decode is weight-bandwidth bound at 2-3 bits. ggml's CPU path already quantizes activations to q8 for its dot products. W8A8 only helps compute-bound GEMMs (prefill), and our weights are 2-3-bit, not 8-bit. Expected gain ~0 (inference).
- **AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization** (MIT-lead; Ji Lin, Jiaming Tang, ..., Song Han). arXiv 2306.00978, MLSys 2024 (best paper). Protects salient weight channels, chosen by activation magnitude, through per-channel scaling before low-bit weight quantization. The TinyChat runtime ships with it. Code: github.com/mit-han-lab/llm-awq. — [arXiv 2306.00978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978)
  - Verdict: the idea is already in GGUF. llama.cpp's imatrix-weighted IQ quantization is an activation-aware importance weighting of the same family. AWQ was designed for INT4/INT3 group-wise uniform quantization and is not competitive with codebook/trellis quantization at ~2-3 bpw (inference; I fetched no AWQ 2-bit numbers). Expected gain: none, or marginal if AWQ scales are folded in before GGUF quantization of attention/shared tensors. Low priority.
- **QServe / QoQ, W4A8KV4** (MIT-lead; Yujun Lin, Haotian Tang, Shang Yang, Zhekai Zhang, Guangxuan Xiao, ..., Song Han; MLSys 2025). arXiv 2405.04532. The weight quantization is progressive: INT8 with per-channel FP16 scales, then INT4 with UINT8 per-group scales, using a protective range of [-119,119] so that INT4 to INT8 dequant runs with register-level parallelism. "SmoothAttention" reduces the accuracy loss of 4-bit KV. Versus TensorRT-LLM, max serving throughput is 1.2x (A100) / 1.4x (L40S) on Llama-3-8B and 2.4x / 3.5x on Qwen1.5-72B, which the paper frames as about 3x lower serving cost. Code: github.com/mit-han-lab/qserve. — [arXiv 2405.04532](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04532); [MLSys 2025 slides](https://mlsys.org/media/mlsys-2025/Slides/3288.pdf); figures vary slightly across arXiv versions per [arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.04532v3)
  - Verdict: W4A8 targets large-batch throughput, so it does not transfer to batch-1 decode. The KV4 part (SmoothAttention, which scales keys to suppress outlier channels) is portable as an idea: q8_0 to a 4-bit KV type would halve KV memory. On a hybrid model, only the full-attention layers keep a KV cache, so the VRAM freed is small. It could still be handed to the expert cache. Expected gain is small: a few % more experts cached at long context (inference, unmeasured).
- **SVDQuant** (MIT-lead; Muyang Li, Yujun Lin, ..., Jun-Yan Zhu, Song Han; ICLR 2025 spotlight). arXiv 2411.05007. W4A4: activation outliers are migrated into the weights, then a high-precision low-rank SVD branch absorbs the weight outliers and a 4-bit branch handles the residual. The Nunchaku engine fuses the two branches. FLUX.1-12B: 3.5x less memory, 3.0x faster than W4A16 on a 16 GB laptop 4090. **Evaluated only on diffusion models.** Code: mit-han-lab/deepcompressor and mit-han-lab/nunchaku. — [arXiv 2411.05007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05007)
  - Verdict: not applicable. Diffusion only and compute-bound W4A4. A low-rank-plus-residual expert format would add a new tensor type for no proven LLM gain.
- **FLUTE: Fast Matrix Multiplications for Lookup Table-Quantized LLMs** (MIT-lead; Han Guo, William Brandon, Radostin Cholakov, Jonathan Ragan-Kelley, Eric Xing, Yoon Kim; EMNLP 2024 Findings). arXiv 2407.10960. A LUT kernel for non-uniform low-bit (e.g. 3-bit) weights. It combines offline weight restructuring, a shared-memory LUT that is vectorized and duplicated to relieve bank pressure, and Stream-K. Reported 2-4x faster than existing GEMM kernels at batch <32, group 128, and 1.5-2x end-to-end throughput on LLaMA-3 with LUT NormalFloat. Code: github.com/HanGuo97/flute. — [arXiv 2407.10960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10960v4); [search summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2407.10960.md)
  - Verdict: partly relevant to the GPU side only. ggml's IQ2_XS/IQ3 kernels are already codebook/LUT dequant kernels. FLUTE's LUT duplication and offline weight re-layout are ideas to check against the HIP mmvq/mmq IQ kernels on RDNA3 (LDS bank conflicts). It does not touch the CPU expert bottleneck. Expected gain: 0-10% on GPU-resident expert matmuls if those kernels prove LUT-bound (inference; profile first).
- **LQ-LoRA** (MIT-lead; Han Guo, ..., Yoon Kim; ICLR 2024). arXiv 2311.12023. Decomposes each matrix into a quantized part plus a low-rank part and uses an integer linear program to pick per-matrix quantization configs under a total memory budget. — [arXiv 2311.12023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12023) (details from memory of the abstract; I did not fetch the page this session)
  - Verdict: the fine-tuning part is irrelevant (we cannot train 125B). The **ILP bit allocation under a byte budget** is a portable offline tool. Choose the GGUF type per tensor or per expert group (e.g. hot experts that stay in VRAM vs cold experts on NVMe) to minimize a sensitivity proxy subject to a VRAM/RAM budget. Expected gain: quality at equal size, not speed (inference).
- KV-cache quantization from MIT Han Lab beyond QServe: the 2026 lab project page lists only "Quant VideoGen" (2-bit KV for autoregressive video diffusion, ICML 2026, up to 7.0x KV memory reduction), which is outside our domain. — [hanlab.mit.edu/projects](https://hanlab.mit.edu/projects)

### Inferences
- At 2-3 bpw, quantizer quality matters more than outlier-smoothing tricks, and MIT's quantizers (AWQ/QoQ) were built for 4-bit and up. The 2-3-bit state of the art the user already runs (IQ/trellis families) comes from other groups (Cornell QuIP#/QTIP, ik_llama), which other reports cover.
- For CPU expert compute, quantization cannot help much further except by going lower in bits, which costs quality. The available lever is fewer bytes read per token, i.e. activation sparsity (Q2).

### Gaps
- I found no MIT paper with GGUF-style or CPU (AVX2) low-bit kernels, and no MIT KV-quant paper for hybrid (GDN plus attention) models.
- I did not fetch AWQ's W3/W2 numbers, so the claim that AWQ is worse than IQ3 at ~3 bpw is an inference from design, not a sourced head-to-head.
- I did not verify whether llm-awq still offers a GGUF/llama.cpp export path.

## Q2. Activation sparsity (TEAL, CATS-like, contextual sparsity) for CPU-computed MoE experts

### Takeaway
TEAL is MIT-led (first author James Liu, MIT; senior Yoon Kim, MIT; Together AI co-authors). It is the strongest candidate, and **it contradicts the Stanford report's preference for CATS**. With greedy per-layer allocation of the threshold calibration, TEAL reaches 40-50% model-wide input sparsity with small loss. At 40% CATS degenerates on Llama-3-8B (PPL ~7.6e4). TEAL has never been tested on MoE or CPU, so the first step is still an offline sparsity measurement on Qwen experts.

### Cited Findings
- **TEAL: Training-Free Activation Sparsity in LLMs** (MIT-lead; James Liu [MIT, intern at Together], Pragaash Ponnusamy [Together], Tianle Cai [Princeton], Han Guo [MIT], Yoon Kim [MIT], Ben Athiwaratkun [Together]). ICLR 2025, arXiv 2408.14690. Code: github.com/FasterDecoding/TEAL. — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3); [ICLR 2025](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/28994)
  - Method: magnitude-thresholds the **input** of all 7 matrices (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down). CATS only sparsifies the MLP. Thresholds come offline from C4 activation distributions. Per-layer sparsities come from block-wise greedy optimization: under 1 A100 GPU-hour for Llama-3-8B with 10 samples of 2048 tokens. — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)
  - Quality (WikiText PPL, dense / 25% / 40% / 50%): Llama-3-8B 5.87 / 5.94 / 6.21 / 6.67. Llama-3-70B 2.93 / 3.02 / 3.52 / 4.30. Mistral-7B 4.92 / 5.01 / 5.13 / 5.31. 6-task average for Llama-3-70B: 80.41 dense, 79.29 at 40%, 78.26 at 50%. Most models degrade sharply at 65%. — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)
  - CATS at 40% gives PPL ~7.6e4 on Llama-3-8B, i.e. it degenerates. TEAL 50% (PPL 6.67) also beats MaskLLM 2:4 weight sparsity (8.53). — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3); this contradicts the Stanford report's choice of CATS as "the best engine idea" ([CATS arXiv 2404.08763](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08763v4))
  - Speed: Triton sparse GEMV kernel (based on DejaVu's), batch 1. Decode speedup at 40%/50%: A6000 Llama-2-7B 1.53x/1.78x, Llama-3-8B 1.42x/1.63x. A100 is lower (1.25-1.40x). At 0% sparsity the kernel is slower than dense (0.91x on A100). — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)
  - Quantization: tested with 8-bit RTN, 4-bit AWQ and **2/3-bit QuIP#** on Llama-2-7B. The PPL break point sits at a similar sparsity across bit widths, i.e. the errors compound roughly independently. No combined sparse+quantized kernel was built. — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)
  - Limits: **no MoE experiments, no CPU experiments**. The authors only remark that low-batch MoE should suffer less from the batching problem. Gains shrink as batch size grows. The LM head is not sparsified. Attention-sink tokens must be protected. — [arXiv 2408.14690v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.14690v3)
- No MIT/Harvard paper applies training-free magnitude activation sparsity inside MoE experts. Nearest non-MIT work: FloE (ICML 2025, arXiv 2505.05950) compresses expert internals and adds sparse prediction for offloaded MoE. It reports 9.3x per-expert compression on Mixtral-8x7B, 11 GB VRAM, up to 48.7x over DeepSpeed-MII on an RTX 3090, at **4.4-7.6% average degradation** (affiliations not shown, not MIT). — [arXiv 2505.05950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05950)

### Inferences
- Portability to our CPU experts: TEAL's sparse input to **down_proj** (the expert intermediate activation) maps directly onto skipping columns of W_down. In GGUF, a row's quant blocks run along the input dimension, so skipping input channels saves bytes only if W_down is re-packed at load time into an n_ff-major / transposed layout. That is the same re-pack the Stanford note proposed for CATS. Sparsifying gate/up input (the hidden state x) is harder: x is shared by all experts and its zeros fall inside 256-element super-blocks. A byte saving there needs whole-block skipping or a channel-reordered layout. Start with down_proj only (and possibly up_proj via a gate-based mask, CATS-style, but with TEAL's per-layer greedy calibration).
- Expected gain, if Qwen experts tolerate ~40% down_proj input sparsity: about 1/3 x 40% ≈ 13% fewer bytes per CPU expert with down-only. Adding gate/up skipping at block granularity could reach 25-40% of CPU expert time. This is an estimate and assumes CPU expert time is purely bandwidth-bound. Quality at 2-3-bit plus sparsity compounds, so TEAL's QuIP# 2/3-bit result is the encouraging but non-MoE data point.
- Key risk: fine-grained MoE experts (small n_ff, already only k-of-N active) may be less sparse than dense 7B MLPs. Measure the |activation| distribution per expert and layer on a coding corpus offline before writing AVX2 kernels. Calibrate on code, not C4, since TEAL calibrated on generic text.
- The same thresholds could also be applied in GPU HIP kernels for cached experts, but TEAL shows the gain is smaller on high-bandwidth hardware (A100 vs A6000), and the GPU is not our bottleneck.

### Gaps
- No published TEAL result on any MoE model, on CPU, or on a GDN hybrid.
- No published sparse+quantized fused kernel to port. TEAL leaves it to future work.

## Q3. MoE efficiency from MIT/Harvard: expert offloading, caching, prefetching, router prediction, pruning, merging

### Takeaway
I found **no MIT-led or Harvard-led paper on expert offloading, caching, prefetching or router prediction**. That literature comes from Edinburgh, SJTU, CMU, Stevens/Rice and others. The two Harvard-led MoE papers found are a theory result (Mixture of Parrots) and a batch-aware routing trick (OEA). Neither speeds up batch-1 decode directly. Parrots does offer a caution for expert pruning on a coding agent.

### Cited Findings
- **Mixture of Parrots: Experts Improve Memorization More Than Reasoning** (Harvard-lead; Samy Jelassi et al., Harvard Kempner, with MIT and Microsoft Research co-authors; ICLR 2025). arXiv 2410.19034. Adding experts keeps improving memorization/knowledge tasks, while reasoning gains saturate. The paper proves some graph-reasoning problems cannot be solved by any number of experts and need a minimum width. MoE matches dense models of equal total parameters on memory-heavy tasks at lower compute. — [arXiv 2410.19034](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.19034v2); [Kempner blog](https://kempnerinstitute.harvard.edu/research/deeper-learning/mixture-of-parrots-experts-improve-memorization-more-than-reasoning/)
- **Opportunistic Expert Activation (OEA): Batch-Aware Expert Routing for Faster Decode Without Retraining** (Harvard-lead; first author Costin-Andrei Oncescu, Harvard, partly as a Together AI intern; co-authors include Tri Dao, Princeton/Together). arXiv 2511.02237, Nov 2025. Re-routes tokens within a batch to minimize the number of unique experts loaded. At batch size 16 it cuts decode latency 39% on a 30B MoE and 15% on a 235B MoE, with no retraining. — [alphaXiv 2511.02237](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.02237)
- The MoE offloading/prefetching search returned only non-MIT work: MoE-Infinity (Edinburgh), ExpertFlow, fMoE (Stevens/Rice), BuddyMoE (SJTU), MoE-SpeQ, SPICE, MELINOE (CMU). None showed MIT affiliation. — [fMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05370v1); [ExpertFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.17954v2); [MoE-SpeQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14102v1); [MoBiLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12357v1)
- The expert pruning/merging search also returned no MIT-affiliated paper (REAP, Sub-MoE, PuzzleMoE, MergeMoE, EvoESAP). REAP (non-MIT) reports that quantization beats expert pruning down to 4-bit, and that below 4-bit, combining quantization with pruning beats more aggressive quantization. — [REAP arXiv 2510.13999](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999v2)

### Inferences
- OEA at batch 1 is a no-op. One niche that may transfer: speculative-decoding verification (and chunked prefill) presents a small batch of tokens per layer. Biasing their routing toward experts already chosen by sibling tokens, or toward experts already cached in VRAM, would reduce CPU misses. "Cache-aware routing" is the generalization, but its quality impact on Qwen is untested and the paper evaluates batch 16 serving, not this use.
- Parrots implies that pruning rarely used experts (REAP-style) to fit more of the model in RAM/VRAM will likely cost **knowledge** (library/API recall, which a coding agent needs) more than reasoning. If pruning is tried, evaluate on code-API recall, not only on reasoning benchmarks. Combined with REAP's finding, below ~3 bpw a mild prune plus a higher-bit quant may beat dropping to IQ2 for everything. That is a testable trade for us (inference).

### Gaps
- No MIT/Harvard work on router prediction or expert prefetching was found, despite targeted searches. The other regional reports should cover this area.
- OEA's venue/acceptance status was not checked.

## Q4. Harvard precision and quantization theory (Pehlevan, Kempner) and practical bit-allocation guidance

### Takeaway
"Scaling Laws for Precision" (Harvard-led; MIT, Stanford and Databricks co-authors) is the key theory result. Post-training-quantization damage **grows with the number of pretraining tokens** and shrinks with model size. Heavily overtrained models like Qwen (trillions of tokens) are therefore unusually fragile at 2-3 bits, which argues for spending extra bits where they matter rather than uniform low bits. I found no 2025-2026 Pehlevan/Bordelon follow-up on quantization.

### Cited Findings
- **Scaling Laws for Precision** (Harvard-lead; Tanishq Kumar [first author, Harvard], Zack Ankner, Benjamin Spector, Blake Bordelon, Niklas Muennighoff, Mansheej Paul, Cengiz Pehlevan, Christopher Ré, Aditi Raghunathan; ICLR 2025). arXiv 2411.04330. A unified functional form for pretraining and post-training precision. — [arXiv 2411.04330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330); [ICLR proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/b2cac94f82928a85055987d9fd44753f-Abstract-Conference.html)
  - PTQ degradation increases with training data D and decreases with model size N and precision. Past a point, more pretraining data makes the post-quantization model worse. — [alphaXiv overview](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2411.04330v2); [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/scaling-laws-for-precision-ai-researcher-sees-perfect-storm-for-the-end-of-scale/)
  - Fitted on 465+ pretraining runs, validated up to 1.7B params / 26B tokens. Compute-optimal training precision is about 7-8 bits (integer) and largely independent of the compute budget. — [arXiv 2411.04330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330); [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/scaling-laws-for-precision-ai-researcher-sees-perfect-storm-for-the-end-of-scale/)
- Independent (non-Harvard) corroboration: "Low-Bit Quantization Favors Undertrained LLMs" (arXiv 2411.17691) finds low-bit damage worse for well-trained models. ParetoQ (arXiv 2502.02631) reports a learning transition between 2 and 3 bits. — [arXiv 2411.17691](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.17691); [arXiv 2502.02631](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2502.02631)
- A targeted search found no 2025-2026 quantization paper by Pehlevan or Bordelon after 2411.04330. — [search results via arXiv 2411.04330 listing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04330)

### Inferences
- Practical guidance for our GGUF:
  1. Keep attention, router/gate, shared-expert and GDN state projections at higher precision (q6/q8). They are a small share of bytes and are read every token.
  2. If bits must be cut, cut cold routed experts first.
  3. Since the 2-to-3-bit range is where quality transitions (ParetoQ), IQ3_KS is likely much safer than IQ2_XS on an overtrained model. That supports using IQ2 only for the coldest (NVMe-tier) experts, with higher bits for the experts that live in VRAM/RAM.
  4. An ILP allocator (LQ-LoRA style, Q1) can formalize this split.
  All four are extrapolations. The scaling law was fitted on ≤1.7B dense models, not on a 125B MoE.
- Scaling Laws for Precision gives no per-tensor sensitivity numbers, so the actual allocation must come from an imatrix/KL-divergence sweep on our own model.

### Gaps
- No Harvard or MIT paper gives MoE-specific precision scaling (per-expert or router sensitivity).
- Kempner Institute affiliation of the Scaling Laws for Precision authors was not explicitly confirmed. The Harvard lead comes from the first author's affiliation.
